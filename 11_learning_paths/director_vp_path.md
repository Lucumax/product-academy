# Director / VP Learning Path

## Target Role
Director of Product, VP of Product — organizational leader responsible for a product portfolio, a team of PMs, and the outcomes of multiple product areas. At Director level, you manage managers (or should). At VP level, you manage Directors or senior managers and may be part of the executive team.

## Prerequisites
- Demonstrated Principal PM-level capability (or completed Principal PM path)
- 8–15+ years of product management experience, typically with 3+ years at Principal or equivalent
- Experience managing or significantly developing other PMs
- Track record of portfolio-level impact (not just single-product impact)

## What Changes at Director/VP Level

The Director transition is the second-hardest in product management (after Senior → Principal). The job changes in several fundamental ways:

1. **From decision quality to decision system quality.** You are no longer primarily evaluated on the quality of your product decisions. You are evaluated on the quality of the decision-making system you create — the forums, criteria, information flows, and culture that produce good decisions across the organization.

2. **From individual multiplication to organizational multiplication.** Principal PMs multiply through frameworks, mentoring, and example. Directors multiply through organizational design, talent systems, and culture — structures that persist and improve without your daily involvement.

3. **From product economics to portfolio economics.** You must allocate resources across initiatives with different risk profiles, time horizons, and return characteristics. Excellence requires comfort with capital allocation logic, not just product logic.

4. **From team communication to executive and board communication.** Your audience changes. You must communicate at altitudes where strategic ambiguity is expensive and where your communications have material consequences for the business.

5. **From building products to building the organization that builds products.** Your primary output is no longer a product or a feature. It's an organization capable of building great products — today, next year, and after you leave.

**Estimated total time:** 180–280 hours cumulative (70–100 additional hours beyond Principal PM path)

## Phase 1: The Director Self-Audit (Weeks 1–2, ~10 hours)

### Week 1: Capability Reassessment
- Re-read `00_orientation/CAPABILITY_MODEL.md` — focus on Director and VP/CPO expectations
- **Critical exercise:** Self-assess against all Director-level capabilities. Use the false positive patterns ruthlessly. The most common failure pattern for Directors is operating as a "super-Principal" — doing more of what made you successful as a Principal rather than developing the qualitatively different capabilities of organizational leadership.
- **External calibration:** Solicit 360 feedback from: (1) your peer Directors in other functions, (2) PMs who report to you, (3) your manager/executive. Use the capability model's level descriptors as the evaluation framework. Compare to your self-assessment.

**Deliverable:** Calibrated self-assessment. Honest acknowledgment of where you're operating as a super-Principal rather than a Director.

### Week 2: Organizational Diagnostic
- **Exercise:** Diagnostic assessment of your product organization. Answer honestly:
  - What percentage of product decisions are escalated to you that shouldn't be?
  - If you were unavailable for a month, which decisions would suffer? Which would be unaffected?
  - What's the capability gap between your best PM and your median PM? Is it shrinking or growing?
  - What's the most important product decision made in your organization this quarter that you didn't personally influence? Was it a good decision?
  - Do PMs in your organization say no to good ideas because they have better ones? Or do they try to do everything?
- **Synthesis:** Write a one-page organizational assessment: what's working, what's broken, what's missing. This becomes the basis for your development priorities.

## Phase 2: Organizational Leadership Capabilities (Weeks 3–12, ~40 hours)

### Week 3: Portfolio Allocation
- Read `03_business_and_gtm/` — portfolio allocation (03.4)
- **Exercise:** Map your current portfolio on two dimensions: (1) expected return, (2) uncertainty/risk. Is your portfolio concentrated or diversified? Where are you over-invested relative to opportunity? Under-invested?
- **Zero-based thinking:** If you were allocating resources from scratch today, with no legacy commitments, what would your portfolio look like? What's different from the current allocation? Why? What would it take to close the gap?
- **Practice:** Identify one initiative in your portfolio that should receive less investment and one that should receive more. Write the investment thesis for each change. Present to your manager or a peer executive.

**Key capability:** Portfolio Allocation

### Week 4: Strategic Sequencing at Scale
- Read `02_principal_plus/` — strategic sequencing (revisit at Director depth)
- **Exercise:** Draw the multi-year capability accumulation path for your portfolio. What capabilities must be built in sequence? Where can you parallelize? What's the bottleneck that determines overall portfolio velocity?
- **Challenge:** Identify your organization's capacity to absorb change. How many significant initiatives can your organization execute simultaneously without quality degradation? Are you currently above or below that threshold?
- **Application:** Write a sequencing recommendation for your portfolio's next 18 months. Include explicit assumptions. Share with your leadership team for debate.

**Key capability:** Strategic Sequencing

### Week 5: Product Organization Design
- Read capability model: Product Organization Design (Section 4.1)
- **Exercise:** Draw your current organizational structure. For each team boundary, identify the coordination cost it creates. For each decision type, identify who actually makes the decision (not who the org chart says). Identify the single organizational change that would most reduce coordination friction.
- **Design exercise:** Design an alternative organizational structure for your portfolio. What would be better about it? What would be worse? What prevents you from implementing it?
- **Application:** If your current structure is suboptimal, write a proposal for change. Include: the current problems, the proposed structure, the transition plan, the risks, and how you'll measure success. Share with your manager.

**Key capability:** Product Organization Design

### Week 6: Capital and Headcount Allocation
- Read `03_business_and_gtm/` — capital and headcount allocation (03.5)
- **Exercise:** Build a resource allocation model for your portfolio. For each initiative: headcount, budget, expected return, uncertainty. What's the marginal return of the last headcount added to each initiative? Where would one additional headcount produce the highest return?
- **Scenario planning:** If you had to cut 20% of your budget, what would you stop? If you received 20% more, what would you start? If neither answer comes easily, your current allocation is probably not strategic.
- **Practice:** Write the annual resource plan for your organization. Include investment theses for each major initiative, not just headcount requests. Present to finance for critique.

**Key capability:** Capital and Headcount Allocation

### Week 7: Talent Development at Scale
- Read capability model: Talent Development (Section 3.3) at Director/VP level
- **Exercise:** Talent review of your product organization. For each PM: current capability level, development edge, expected trajectory, retention risk. Where's your bench strength? Where are you vulnerable to departure?
- **Practice:** Have one hard developmental conversation this week — a conversation about performance that you've been avoiding, or honest feedback that's uncomfortable to deliver. Prepare. Deliver directly. Follow up.
- **System design:** What's your system for developing PMs? If the answer is "I mentor them," you don't have a system — you have a personal practice that doesn't scale. Design a development system that works without you.

**Key capability:** Talent Development

### Week 8: Organizational Multiplication II
- Read capability model: Organizational Multiplication (Section 3.2) at Director/VP level
- **Exercise:** Audit your organization's decision-making. What decisions require your personal involvement? For each, answer: Why? Can this decision right be delegated? What would need to be true to delegate it? What's the cost of not delegating?
- **Practice:** Delegate one significant decision this month that you would normally make yourself. Provide context, criteria, and boundaries. Don't override the decision unless it would cause material harm. Learn from the outcome — including if you were wrong about the need to be involved.
- **System improvement:** Identify one organizational process (planning, reviews, decision forums) that creates overhead without improving decisions. Redesign or eliminate it.

**Key capability:** Organizational Multiplication

### Week 9: Executive Communication II
- Read capability model: Executive Communication (Section 2.3) at Director/VP level
- **Exercise:** Audit your last 5 executive communications. For each: Did the executive make a decision based on your communication? Was it the decision you intended? If not, why?
- **Practice:** Write a board-level memo for your product area. What would you want the board to know? What would you want them to decide? What would you want them to worry about? Share with your manager or a peer executive for critique.
- **Feedback:** Ask a C-suite executive you work with: "What could I do differently in my communications to you to make your decisions easier?" Their answer may surprise you.

**Key capability:** Executive Communication

### Week 10: Cross-Functional Leadership
- Read capability model: Cross-Functional Influence (Section 2.2) at Director/VP level
- **Exercise:** Map your peer relationships with Directors/VPs in other functions (engineering, design, sales, marketing, finance). For each: Is this a productive partnership where you make each other better? Is there unresolved tension? What would improve it?
- **Practice:** Identify the peer relationship that most affects your organization's effectiveness. Invest in improving it this month — joint planning session, honest conversation about tensions, shared commitment to a specific outcome.
- **Intervention:** Identify a cross-functional dynamic that's blocking your organization. Design and execute an intervention. Measure whether it improved.

**Key capability:** Cross-Functional Influence

### Week 11: Outcome Ownership at Scale
- Read capability model: Outcome Ownership (Section 2.4) at Director/VP level
- **Exercise:** For each team or product area in your portfolio: What outcomes are they accountable for? Are they measurable? Are they the right outcomes? Are teams actually held accountable — or are misses explained away?
- **Audit:** Review the outcomes your organization committed to 6–12 months ago. Which were achieved? Which weren't? For those that weren't, was the explanation honest and specific? Was there learning and adjustment?
- **System design:** What's your system for organizational outcome accountability? If the answer is "quarterly business reviews," that's not a system — it's a meeting. Design a system that creates genuine accountability without creating fear or gaming.

**Key capability:** Outcome Ownership

### Week 12: Adoption and Change Management at Scale
- Read capability model: Adoption and Change Management (Section 3.1) at Director/VP level
- **Exercise:** For your portfolio's most important products: Is the organization structured to achieve adoption, or just to build? Where is the adoption responsibility? Is it working?
- **Audit:** Review products launched in the past 12 months. What percentage achieved target adoption? For those that didn't, was the issue product quality or adoption execution? What's the pattern?
- **Organizational design:** Should adoption capability live in product, in go-to-market, or in a dedicated function? What's right for your products and market? Make a deliberate decision rather than defaulting to historical practice.

**Key capability:** Adoption and Change Management

## Phase 3: Breadth and Depth (Weeks 13–18, ~25 hours)

### Week 13: Advance Business Capabilities
- Read `03_business_and_gtm/` — build vs. buy vs. partner (03.6)
- Read `03_business_and_gtm/` — board-level narrative (03.7) at Director depth
- **Exercise:** For each significant build-vs-buy decision your organization faces (or has faced), write the analysis you should have done. What would a rigorous build-vs-buy analysis include? What assumptions matter most?

### Week 14–15: Contradictions Deep Dive
- Read `08_contradictions/` — all modules relevant to your current challenges
- **Minimum:** Speed vs. Quality (08.1), Autonomy vs. Alignment (08.2), Innovation vs. Execution (08.5), Short-Term vs. Long-Term (08.9), Centralization vs. Decentralization (08.7)
- **Exercise:** For each contradiction, identify a specific instance in your organization. How are you currently resolving it? Is your resolution deliberate or accidental? What would the alternative resolution look like?
- **Discussion:** Discuss 2–3 contradictions with peer Directors. Where do you differ? Why? The disagreements are often more instructive than the agreements.

### Week 16: Industry Overlays
- Complete at least 2 industry overlay modules from Track 06 that are relevant to your context or that you want to understand better.
- **Exercise:** For each overlay, identify how your product leadership approach would need to change if your company operated in that context. What would be easier? Harder? Different?

### Week 17: AI Product Leadership (select modules)
- Complete At minimum: AI Strategy and Narrative (05.7), AI Organization Design (05.6)
- If your products involve AI, complete additional modules as relevant.
- **Exercise:** Write your organization's AI product strategy in one page. What's your build-vs-use posture? What's your data advantage (if any)? What's your AI governance approach? If you can't answer these, your organization has an AI strategy gap.

### Week 18: Integration
- **Synthesis exercise:** Write a comprehensive strategy document for your product portfolio: thesis, sequencing, resource allocation, organizational design, key risks, success metrics. 5 pages maximum. Share with your manager and peer executives for critique.

## Phase 4: Cases, Simulation, and Assessment (Weeks 19–22, ~20 hours)

### Week 19–20: Case Work
- Complete `07_cases/` — Organizational Leadership Cases (07.3) and Institutional Leadership Cases (07.4)
- **Exercise:** For each case, write your decision before reading the analysis. Focus especially on cases where the "right" answer depends on context that you might have missed.
- **Discussion:** Discuss cases with peer Directors. Calibrate judgment. Pay attention to cases where you disagree — those reveal differences in mental models.

### Week 21: Simulator
- Complete `10_simulator/` — Director Scenarios (10.3) and Organizational Change Scenarios (10.6)
- **Exercise:** For each simulator scenario, document your decision process, not just your decision. After feedback, identify: What did you consider? What did you miss? What pattern does this reveal about your judgment?

### Week 22: Assessment and Planning
- **Reassessment:** Self-assess against all Director-level capabilities. Compare to your Phase 1 assessment. Where have you improved? Where haven't you? Why?
- **360 follow-up:** Re-engage your Phase 1 external calibrators. Ask: "What have you observed about my development in the past 6 months? What am I still missing?"
- **Development plan:** Create a 12-month development plan. Director development is measured in years, not weeks. The 3 capabilities you're weakest in today will likely still be your development priorities a year from now. Commit to sustained practice.
- **Assessment gate:** When ready, attempt the Director Quality Gate.

## Key Capabilities by Priority for Directors/VPs

1. **Portfolio Allocation** — The most common gap for new Directors. You must allocate resources across initiatives with different profiles. This is capital allocation thinking, not just product thinking. Most new Directors underinvest in the best opportunities and overinvest in the most familiar ones.

2. **Product Organization Design** — The structure of your organization determines which products get built, which decisions get made, and which problems get attention. Most Directors inherit an organizational structure and optimize within it. Great Directors redesign it.

3. **Talent Development at Scale** — Your primary constraint is no longer your own capability. It's the capability of your organization. Directors who don't systematically develop talent will hit a ceiling determined by their personal capacity.

4. **Organizational Multiplication** — The Director who makes every important decision personally is the bottleneck, not the solution. Your job is to create the conditions for good decisions to be made without you.

5. **Executive and Board Communication** — You are now communicating at altitudes where your words have material consequences. Excellence in executive communication is not about polish — it's about clarity, accuracy, and the courage to deliver uncomfortable truths.

6. **Cross-Functional Leadership** — You can no longer succeed by being a great PM. You succeed by building productive partnerships with peer leaders in other functions. The quality of those relationships determines the quality of your organization's output.

## Signs You're Ready for the VP/CPO Path

- Your organization makes good product decisions without your involvement in most of them
- You've successfully redesigned your organizational structure at least once in response to strategy change
- You can articulate your portfolio allocation thesis and trace resource deployment to it
- Your peer executives seek your input on company-level decisions beyond product
- You've developed at least one PM who has been promoted to Director or equivalent
- Your organization's median PM is more capable than they were two years ago
- Your self-assessment shows "Proficient" or "Excellent" on all Director-level capabilities

## Signs You're Not Ready

- You're still the primary product decision-maker for your organization
- Your organizational structure is the one you inherited
- You can't articulate why resources are allocated the way they are
- You haven't had a difficult talent conversation in the past 6 months
- Your cross-functional relationships are cordial but not strategically productive
- You spend more than 50% of your time on activities a Principal PM could do
- Your organization would be materially worse at product development if you left for a month

## The Director's Paradox

The Director transition is paradoxical in its own way: your value is measured by what happens when you're not in the room. If every good decision requires your presence, you're not a Director — you're a super-Principal with a Director title. If your organization doesn't get better at product development over time, you're not developing talent — you're consuming it.

The Director who is indispensable to daily decisions has failed at the job. The Director who has made themselves unnecessary to daily decisions — while the organization produces better outcomes than ever — has succeeded. Your goal is to work yourself out of every job except the ones only you can do: setting direction, building the organization, and managing the interfaces with the rest of the company.

---

*This path assumes the Principal PM foundation. If you discover gaps at the Principal level during Phase 1, address them. A Director operating on a weak Principal foundation will make organizational decisions that look good on an org chart and fail in practice.*
