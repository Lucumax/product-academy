# Scenario 10: Services Revenue vs. Product Development

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|

| **title** | Services Revenue vs. Product Development |
| **leadership_level** | Director, VP Product, CPO, Founder |
| **primary_tension** | Short-term revenue vs. long-term defensibility |
| **key_capability** | Founder/product strategy and business model choice |
| **estimated_time** | 45 minutes |
| **related_principles** | PRN-0002 (Strategy Is What You Say No To), PRN-0010 (Economic Moats), PRN-0012 (Founder Product Leadership) |

## Situation

You are the **Founder and Product Leader** of **Athena Compliance**, a startup building software that helps mid-size companies manage regulatory compliance workflows. Your product automates the creation, review, approval, and audit of compliance documentation — think SOC 2 evidence collection, GDPR data mapping, HIPAA risk assessments, ISO 27001 control monitoring.

The company is 2.5 years old. You have 11 employees, $2.1M in seed funding (18 months ago), and you are burning $110K/month. Your current runway is **7 months**. You have $18K in annual recurring revenue from 4 early customers who are using an MVP version of the product.

### The Services Opportunity

Six months ago, a former colleague who is now CISO at a $200M fintech company asked if you could help them with their SOC 2 Type II audit preparation. You said yes because you needed cash and the relationship. You charged $45K for a 6-week engagement. You, personally, did most of the work — mapping their controls, writing evidence collection procedures, preparing auditor documentation.

The engagement went extremely well. The client was thrilled. They referred you to two other CISOs in their network. Suddenly, you have three inbound requests for compliance consulting engagements:

- **Engagement A:** SOC 2 Type II preparation for a Series B startup. $60K, 8 weeks.
- **Engagement B:** GDPR compliance program for a company expanding to EU. $75K, 10 weeks.
- **Engagement C:** ISO 27001 certification prep for a health-tech company. $90K, 12 weeks.

Total: $225K in services revenue over the next 3-4 months. That's more than your current annual product revenue ($18K) multiplied by 10.

If you take all three engagements, you can:
- Extend your runway from 7 months to 14+ months (the services revenue covers burn plus a small surplus)
- Build deep expertise in the compliance workflows that your product is supposed to automate
- Generate case studies and testimonials from real clients
- Potentially convert services clients into product customers ("you just went through SOC 2 with us — now use our software to maintain it")

All three potential clients know you're a product company. They're hiring you specifically for your compliance expertise, not your software.

### The Product Reality

Your product MVP has 4 customers and generates $18K ARR. It works for basic compliance tracking but is missing critical capabilities:
- Automated evidence collection (the product shows you what evidence you need but doesn't collect it from source systems)
- Multi-framework support (a customer doing SOC 2 AND GDPR has to duplicate work across two separate instances)
- Auditor collaboration (the product doesn't have a portal for external auditors to review evidence)
- Integration with common infrastructure tools (AWS, GitHub, Jira, Okta)

Building these capabilities requires focused engineering investment. Your team:
- You (Founder/CEO): product strategy, client relationships, some delivery
- CTO (Mikhail): 15 years engineering experience, can build anything but is one person
- 2 full-stack engineers: solid, mid-level, can execute on well-defined tasks
- 1 designer (part-time, 20 hours/week)
- 1 compliance domain expert (your co-founder, Priya, who does the client delivery on services work)
- 3 customer-facing roles (sales, support, customer success — all wearing multiple hats)
- 2 operations/admin

If you take the services engagements, you (founder) will spend 60-70% of your time on client delivery for the next 3-4 months. So will Priya. That time will not be spent on product strategy, customer discovery, or building the product roadmap and go-to-market.

Mikhail has been politely persistent: "I understand the need for cash. But every month we spend doing services is a month we're not building the product. The product is what makes this a company, not a consultancy. If we're going to be a consultancy, let's be honest about that and stop pretending we're a product company."

### The Deeper Strategic Question

This is not just a "should we do some consulting on the side?" question. It's a business model question:

**Product companies and services companies are different organisms.** They have different:
- Cost structures (product: high fixed cost, low marginal cost; services: low fixed cost, high marginal cost)
- Growth dynamics (product: slow initial growth, accelerating once PMF is achieved; services: linear growth, limited by headcount)
- Defensibility (product: IP, network effects, switching costs; services: relationships, reputation, expertise)
- Valuations (product: 8-15x ARR; services: 1-3x revenue)
- Investor expectations (your seed investors invested in a product company, not a consultancy)

Many product companies start with services. Some successfully transition (Basecamp started as a web design consultancy; GitHub started doing custom development). Many get stuck — the services revenue becomes a drug, and the product never achieves escape velocity. The company becomes a "product-enabled consultancy" or a "consultancy with a software tool" — a fine business, but not what you raised venture capital to build.

### What Your Investors Think

Your lead investor, **Katherine Okonkwo** at NextView Ventures, invested in a product company. In your last board update, you mentioned the SOC 2 engagement and the pipeline of services work. Her response was measured but clear:

"Services revenue is a bridge, not a destination. It can extend runway while you find product-market fit, but it cannot replace product-market fit. Every services engagement should have a clear hypothesis about how it advances the product. If you're still 50%+ services in 12 months, we need to have a different conversation about what kind of company this is."

Translation: "Proceed with caution. We invested in a product company. Don't become a consultancy."

### What Your Co-Founder Thinks

Priya (your co-founder and compliance domain expert) is torn. She sees the services work as validating:
- The demand is real (clients are paying real money for compliance help)
- The workflow is real (she's doing the work manually that the product should automate)
- The expertise is valuable (clients trust you because you deeply understand compliance, not just software)

But she also sees the risk:
- "Every hour I spend on client delivery is an hour I'm not spending on product discovery. I'm learning about individual clients' compliance needs, not about the patterns across clients that would make a product valuable."
- "The clients who hire us for services are not necessarily the same as the clients who would buy a product. They have budget for consulting. They may not have budget for software."

### The Market Context

The compliance software market is growing fast. Vanta and Drata (SOC 2 automation) raised significant funding and are growing rapidly. Secureframe and Thoropass are also well-funded competitors. They are building product companies. They are not doing services.

If you spend the next 12 months doing services while they spend the next 12 months building product, the competitive gap will widen. You'll know more about compliance consulting, but they'll have better software. In a market where AI is starting to automate compliance workflows, a services-heavy approach may be betting against technology trends.

## Characters

**Mikhail (CTO, Co-Founder).** Your technical co-founder. Joined because he wanted to build a product that automates compliance. Increasingly worried that the company is drifting toward services. Has said: "I didn't leave my FAANG job to run a consulting firm. If we're doing services, I need to know so I can decide whether this is still the right place for me." Motivated by: building technology, product impact, professional identity.

**Priya (Co-Founder, Compliance Domain Expert).** Your domain expert co-founder. Former compliance officer at a large bank. The person who actually delivers the services work. Is learning a ton from client engagements but also burning out from doing everything manually. Motivated by: solving compliance pain, building a real company, not burning out on client delivery.

**Katherine Okonkwo (Lead Investor).** Partner at NextView Ventures. Invested in your vision of "automating compliance for the mid-market." Supportive but watching the services-vs-product mix carefully. Has seen multiple portfolio companies get stuck in services. Motivated by: fund returns, not having a portfolio company become a zombie (alive but not growing fast enough to matter).

**Your 4 Product Customers (external).** Early adopters who are using the MVP. They're patient but not infinitely so. They signed up for a product company that will improve the software. If you disappear into services for 6 months, they'll notice that the product isn't getting better.

**The Services Clients (external).** Three companies who want to hire you for compliance consulting. They don't care about your product vision. They care about getting their SOC 2/GDPR/ISO 27001 certification done well and on time. They're paying for expertise and execution, not software.

## Constraints

- 7 months of runway. Without additional revenue, the company runs out of money.
- $18K ARR from product. Not nearly enough to cover $110K/month burn.
- The team is small (11 people). You cannot spin up a separate services division without hiring, which you can't afford.
- You and Priya are the only people who can deliver the services work. If you both do services, product development stops.
- Your product competitors (Vanta, Drata) are well-funded and building fast. Time matters.
- Your investors invested in a product company. A pivot to services requires a conversation.

## Your Role

You are the Founder and product leader. You own the strategy, the fundraising, the team, and the product. Every decision is yours. Your co-founders (Mikhail and Priya) need you to lead. Your investors need you to be clear about what kind of company you're building. Your team needs you to provide direction and stability.

## Response Format

### Part 1: Assumptions

Key areas: Can the services work be systematized so it doesn't depend on you and Priya personally? Will services clients convert to product customers, or are they separate markets? How long is the product development path to $1M ARR? Is it possible to do BOTH services and product well with 11 people? What does Mikhail actually need from you to stay? What would Katherine need to see to support a services-heavy interim strategy?

### Part 2: Decision

Describe your strategy with:
- **The business model decision.** Are you a product company that does some services? A services company that builds product? Something else? Be explicit.
- **What services you will take (and not take).** Which of the three engagements, and why?
- **What product work continues.** What does Mikhail's team build while you and Priya are on services delivery?
- **The transition plan.** If you do services now, what is the plan to transition back to product? What has to be true for the transition to happen? When?
- **The investor conversation.** What do you tell Katherine? What commitments do you make?
- **The co-founder conversation.** What do you tell Mikhail? Priya?

### Part 3: Pre-Mortem

Assume your strategy failed. 18 months from now, the company has $400K in services revenue and $25K in product ARR. You are a successful small consultancy and a failed product company. Write a specific pre-mortem. At least 3 failure paths. Include: what made the services revenue too attractive to give up, what product milestones you missed, and what conversations you avoided having.

## Scoring Rubric (Scenario-Specific)

### Business Model Clarity

| Score | What We Look For |
|-------|-----------------|
| 1-2 | No clarity on the business model. "We'll do services AND product" without recognizing the tension between them. |
| 3 | Articulates a clear position: "We are a product company. Services are a bridge to product revenue. The bridge has a defined end point." OR "We are pivoting to a services-led model because [clear rationale]." Either position is valid if clearly reasoned. |
| 4 | Defines the transition conditions explicitly: "We will take the 3 services engagements to extend runway to 14 months. During those 14 months, the product must reach $100K ARR. If it doesn't, we will [specific alternative]. If it does, we will stop taking new services engagements and invest fully in product." |
| 5 | Designs a hybrid model that is strategic, not accidental: "Services is our R&D. Every engagement teaches us something about the compliance automation workflow. We will capture those learnings systematically and feed them into the product. After every engagement, Mikhail's team builds the feature that would have automated the most painful part of that engagement. By engagement #10, the product automates 80% of what we do manually. At that point, we transition from services to product — not because we decided to, but because the product is good enough that clients buy it instead of hiring us." |

### Resource Allocation and Trade-offs

| Score | What We Look For |
|-------|-----------------|
| 1-2 | Assumes you can do everything — all three engagements AND full product development AND fundraising. No acknowledgment of the 11-person constraint. |
| 3 | Makes explicit trade-offs: if you take the services engagements, what product work stops? Who does what? What is the minimum product investment that must continue? |
| 4 | Creates a resource allocation model over time: Month 1-4 (services heavy, product maintenance only), Month 5-8 (services declining, product ramping up), Month 9-14 (product heavy, services by exception only for strategic clients). Each phase has a staffing plan. |
| 5 | Identifies the resource that is the actual bottleneck (likely you, the founder) and designs the allocation to maximize the company's long-term value, not just this quarter's revenue. If you are the bottleneck, you should spend your time on the highest-leverage activity — which might be product strategy and fundraising, not client delivery. Considers hiring a services delivery lead so you and Priya can step back from delivery. |

### Co-Founder and Team Alignment

| Score | What We Look For |
|-------|-----------------|
| 1-2 | Makes the decision unilaterally. Doesn't address Mikhail's expressed concern about becoming a consultancy. |
| 3 | Has explicit conversations with both co-founders. Addresses Mikhail's concern with a specific commitment: "We are a product company. Here's the plan to stay one." |
| 4 | Involves co-founders in designing the approach: "Mikhail, here's the constraint — we have 7 months of runway. Here's the services opportunity — $225K. Here's the product trajectory — $18K ARR with a path to $100K but it takes 12 months. How do we solve this together?" Makes the decision a shared one, not an imposed one. |
| 5 | Creates a shared narrative that all three co-founders believe in: "We're going to use services to (a) extend runway, (b) learn the compliance workflow at a level of depth our competitors can't match, and (c) build a product that automates the most painful parts of what we're doing manually. In 24 months, we'll have deeper domain expertise than any competitor because we've DONE the work, not just built software for it. That's our moat." Uses Mikhail's technical ambition and Priya's domain expertise as the foundation of the strategy. |

### Investor Management

| Score | What We Look For |
|-------|-----------------|
| 1-2 | Hides the services work from investors. Or presents it without acknowledging investor concerns. |
| 3 | Communicates transparently with Katherine: "We're taking services work to extend runway. Here's how it advances the product. Here's when we transition back. Here's what we need from you." |
| 4 | Presents a data-driven case: "Katherine, we have 7 months of runway and a product at $18K ARR. If we raise now, we raise from weakness. If we use services to extend runway to 14 months and grow product to $100K ARR, we raise from strength. The services work is buying us the time to make the product investable. Here's the monthly dashboard I'll share with you to track product vs. services revenue." |
| 5 | Aligns investor incentives with the strategy: "Katherine, the services work is generating cash that reduces our burn rate and extends our runway without dilution. It's also generating domain expertise that makes our product moat deeper. If we can reach $100K product ARR with services-funded runway, our Series A valuation is higher and your ownership is more valuable. If we can't reach $100K ARR in 14 months, we'll have a candid conversation about whether the product opportunity is real." |

## Facilitator Notes

**Common traps:**
1. Taking all three services engagements without a clear plan to exit services. This is how product companies become consultancies — the money is good, the work is satisfying, and the product roadmap keeps slipping.
2. Refusing all services work on principle. The company has 7 months of runway and $18K ARR. Raising another round with those numbers is extremely difficult. The services opportunity is real cash that buys time.
3. Pretending the team can do both. 11 people cannot simultaneously deliver 3 consulting engagements, build a product, and raise a round. Something will suffer. Be explicit about what.
4. Not having the hard conversation with Mikhail. If he joined to build a product and you're doing services, his frustration is legitimate. Address it directly.

**Discussion prompts:**
- At what point does a product company become a consultancy? Is it 50% revenue from services? 50% of team time? Something else?
- If you were Katherine (the investor), what would make you comfortable with a services-heavy interim period?
- Is there a version of the services work that accelerates the product instead of delaying it?
- What would you do if Mikhail gave you an ultimatum: "If we take all three services engagements, I'm leaving"?
- Is a $225K services business a better outcome than a failed product company? Under what circumstances?
