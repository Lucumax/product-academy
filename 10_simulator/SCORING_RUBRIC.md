# Scoring Rubric

## Purpose

This rubric evaluates scenario responses across five leadership levels and eleven dimensions. It is designed to reward judgment and context sensitivity, not framework completeness. A response that applies a framework mechanically without adapting to the scenario's specific context should score lower than one that shows contextual reasoning even if less structured.

## The Core Principle

> A Senior PM can tell you the right framework. A Principal can tell you which framework and why. A Director can tell you when the framework is wrong and what to use instead. A VP can tell you when no framework helps and you must rely on judgment. An executive can create the conditions where the right decision gets made regardless of who makes it.

## Scoring Dimensions

Each dimension is scored 1-5. The definitions below describe what good looks like at each level, not the minimum required for that level.

---

### 1. Problem Framing

*Can you define the real problem, not just the presenting symptoms?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | Restates the scenario description without reframing. No distinction between symptoms and root causes. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Applies a standard problem framing (e.g., "this is a prioritization problem") without interrogating whether the frame fits. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Identifies the type of problem (resource allocation, strategy, organizational, ethical) and connects it to the right analytical approach. |
| 4 | Principal | Distinguishes between the presenting problem and underlying systemic issue. Reframes the problem in a way that opens up new solution spaces. |
| 5 | Director+ | Identifies how the problem is connected to organizational design, incentive structures, or strategic positioning. Redefines the problem in terms that the executive team would recognize as the actual problem. |

---

### 2. Stakeholder Analysis

*Can you map who has power, who has interest, and who will resist?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | Lists the characters by name with no analysis of their interests or power. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Categorizes stakeholders (high/low power, high/low interest) but doesn't specify what they want or why. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Identifies each stakeholder's explicit goals, latent concerns, and likely resistance points. Shows understanding of how stakeholders influence the decision. |
| 4 | Principal | Maps stakeholder coalitions — who will ally with whom, who has informal power despite low formal authority. Identifies who must actively support the decision vs. who needs to merely not block it. |
| 5 | Director+ | Shows how the organizational structure and incentive systems create the stakeholder positions. Identifies the stakeholder whose incentives must change for the solution to work long-term. Anticipates second-order stakeholder effects. |

---

### 3. Incentive Mapping

*Can you articulate what each stakeholder wants and fears?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | Assumes stakeholders want "the best product" or "the company to succeed." No recognition of misaligned incentives. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Lists stated goals without distinguishing between stated goals and actual incentives. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Distinguishes between stated goals and actual incentives. Identifies misalignments and conflicts. |
| 4 | Principal | Connects incentives to compensation structures, performance metrics, and career incentives. Identifies who is rewarded for what behavior and how that shapes their position. |
| 5 | Director+ | Proposes how to change the incentive structure to align stakeholders rather than working around existing misalignments. Identifies the upstream organizational decision that created the incentive conflict. |

---

### 4. Alternatives Generation

*Can you generate and evaluate multiple distinct options, not just two?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | Presents one option (usually the most obvious one) or a false binary ("do it or don't"). |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Generates 2-3 options but they are variations on the same theme. No orthogonal alternatives. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Generates 3+ genuinely distinct options with different resource commitments, sequencing, and risk profiles. Evaluates each on multiple criteria. |
| 4 | Principal | Generates options that operate on different levels — options that change the constraints, options that change the timeline, options that change who decides. Includes "uncomfortable" alternatives that are politically difficult but analytically sound. |
| 5 | Director+ | Generates options that change the system — options that restructure the team, renegotiate commitments, or reframe the problem for leadership. Includes the option of making the trade-off explicit and asking executives to own it. |

---

### 5. Evidence Planning

*Can you specify what evidence would change your mind and how you'd get it?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | No evidence plan. Relies entirely on assumptions and reasoning. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Mentions "we should gather data" without specifying what data or how. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Identifies specific evidence gaps, proposes methods to close them, and estimates the cost and time of doing so. |
| 4 | Principal | Prioritizes evidence gaps by decision impact — which unknowns, if resolved, would most change the decision. Designs evidence gathering that is proportional to the decision's irreversibility. |
| 5 | Director+ | Identifies evidence that the organization systematically fails to gather because of incentive structures. Proposes measurement systems that would change how the decision is evaluated over time. |

---

### 6. Decision Quality

*Is the decision specific, allocative, and connected to a rationale?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | Vague aspiration ("we'll find a way to do both"). No specific actions or resource commitments. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Specific actions but no trade-offs — tries to satisfy all stakeholders without acknowledging what is being sacrificed. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Clear decision with explicit trade-offs. Connects the decision to a rationale. Acknowledges what is being deferred or sacrificed. |
| 4 | Principal | Decision includes sequencing, dependencies, and conditions. Specifies what will NOT be done and why. Includes a communication plan for the trade-off. |
| 5 | Director+ | Decision is framed as an experiment with a defined evaluation period, success criteria, and explicit reversal conditions. The decision logic is communicated in terms that each key stakeholder understands. |

---

### 7. Resource Allocation

*Does the decision specify who does what, when, and what they stop doing?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | No resource allocation specified. Assumes resources are infinite or will "figure it out." |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Allocates people to work without specifying what they will stop doing. Adds to capacity without recognizing opportunity cost. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Specifies who works on what and what they stop doing. Estimates person-weeks or person-months. Recognizes capacity constraints. |
| 4 | Principal | Allocates not just execution capacity but also decision-making attention — who decides what, who is consulted, who is informed. Protects team attention from competing demands. |
| 5 | Director+ | Identifies the organizational bottleneck (not just team-level capacity) and addresses it. Considers whether the resource constraint is a signal that strategy needs to change rather than just a resource allocation problem. |

---

### 8. Metrics and Counter-Metrics

*Do you define success AND failure signals, not just success?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | No metrics specified or generic metrics ("revenue," "user satisfaction") without operationalization. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Defines success metrics but no counter-metrics. Assumes if success metric moves in the right direction, everything is fine. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Defines both success metrics and counter-metrics — metrics that should NOT move in the wrong direction. Distinguishes between leading and lagging indicators. |
| 4 | Principal | Identifies metrics that stakeholders will game and proposes safeguards. Defines metrics that would signal the decision was wrong even if all success metrics look good. |
| 5 | Director+ | Defines a metrics system that creates accountability for the trade-off — e.g., if we sacrifice quality for speed, the quality metric has an owner and a threshold that triggers escalation. |

---

### 9. Pre-Mortem Quality

*Are the failure paths specific, mechanistic, and monitorable?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | No pre-mortem or generic failure mode ("the market changed," "the team couldn't execute"). |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Lists 2-3 generic risks without explaining the mechanism of failure or how to detect it. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Identifies specific failure mechanisms with causal chains. Describes what would happen step by step. Defines early warning signals for each. |
| 4 | Principal | Identifies failure modes that are likely given the specific constraints, characters, and organizational dynamics. Includes political/organizational failure modes, not just product/technical ones. |
| 5 | Director+ | Identifies systemic failure modes — failures that would occur regardless of who executes the decision because of structural or incentive problems. Specifies who would detect each failure mode and how quickly. |

---

### 10. Reversal Conditions

*Do you specify what evidence would trigger a change in approach?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | No reversal conditions. Decision is presented as final and unconditional. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Generic reversal conditions ("if it's not working, we'll try something else") without specifying what "not working" means or when the check happens. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Specific, measurable reversal conditions with a defined check-in cadence. Specifies what data triggers reconsideration. |
| 4 | Principal | Reversal conditions are designed to detect the specific failure modes identified in the pre-mortem. Includes both metric triggers and qualitative triggers (e.g., "if the engineering lead flags that architectural debt is becoming unmanageable"). |
| 5 | Director+ | Reversal conditions are communicated to stakeholders as part of the decision. The process for revisiting the decision is specified (who decides, what forum, what data is required). Reversal is treated as a design feature, not a failure. |

---

### 11. Executive Communication

*Can you communicate the decision's logic and trade-offs concisely?*

| Score | Level | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Novice | Cannot articulate the decision in terms an executive would find useful. Too much detail or too vague. |
| 2 | Structured but Shallow | Communicates the decision but not the trade-off. Focuses on what will be done without acknowledging the cost. |
| 3 | Strong Senior PM | Communicates the decision, the rationale, and the trade-off. Anticipates the executive's most likely question and answers it preemptively. |
| 4 | Principal | Frames the decision in terms of the executive's concerns (revenue risk, strategic coherence, competitive positioning, organizational health). Communicates the trade-off as a strategic choice, not an operational detail. |
| 5 | Director+ | Recommends the decision while making the alternatives and their consequences visible to the executive. Creates the conditions for the executive to make the decision themselves if it exceeds the recommender's authority. The communication itself is a decision-support tool. |

## Level Descriptions

### Structured but Shallow (Total Score: 11-22)

**What it looks like:**
- Applies frameworks (RICE, SWOT, 2x2 matrices) correctly but mechanically
- Identifies the "right" answer from a textbook but misses contextual factors that make it wrong
- Lists stakeholders without understanding what they actually want
- Generates alternatives from a standard playbook (build, buy, partner) without scenario-specific creativity
- Pre-mortem is generic ("the project is late," "the market changes")

**How to improve:**
- Before applying a framework, ask: "What about this situation makes the standard approach inadequate?"
- For each stakeholder, ask: "What do they lose if my decision succeeds? What do they gain if my decision fails?"
- Practice writing pre-mortems that name specific people making specific decisions, not abstract forces

### Strong Senior PM (Total Score: 23-33)

**What it looks like:**
- Adapts frameworks to the specific context rather than applying them rigidly
- Shows understanding of stakeholder motivations beyond stated goals
- Generates multiple genuinely distinct options
- Defines both success and counter-metrics
- Pre-mortem identifies specific failure mechanisms with causal chains
- Reversal conditions are measurable and time-bound

**How to improve toward Principal:**
- Move from identifying stakeholder conflicts to proposing mechanisms that resolve them
- Add systems thinking: what organizational structure or incentive system created this problem?
- Frame decisions as experiments with defined learning objectives
- Practice communicating upward — can your VP make a decision based on your analysis without reading the full document?

### Principal (Total Score: 34-44)

**What it looks like:**
- Reframes the problem in a way that opens new solution spaces
- Identifies the organizational design or incentive system that created the situation
- Generates options that operate at different levels (operational, organizational, strategic)
- Designs evidence gathering proportional to decision irreversibility
- Pre-mortem identifies systemic failure modes, not just execution failures
- Reversal conditions are specific to the failure mechanisms identified in the pre-mortem
- Communication frames the decision as a strategic choice for leadership, not an operational recommendation

**How to improve toward Director:**
- Move from "what decision should we make?" to "who should make this decision and what conditions do they need?"
- Identify which organizational processes need to change to prevent this situation from recurring
- Design metrics systems (not just metrics) — who owns what metric, what happens when thresholds are breached

### Director (Total Score: 45-50)

**What it looks like:**
- Identifies how the problem is connected to organizational design and long-term strategy
- Shows how the decision affects multiple teams, not just the immediate team
- Generates options that restructure the way decisions are made, not just what decisions are made
- Defines metrics systems with ownership, accountability, and escalation paths
- Pre-mortem identifies failures that would occur regardless of who executes
- Reversal conditions are communicated to stakeholders and incorporated into organizational processes

**How to improve toward Executive:**
- Move from "what decision should we make?" to "what conditions would make the right decision inevitable?"
- Identify the upstream decisions (hiring, org structure, funding model) that created the constraints
- Practice making the trade-off explicit and asking higher-ups to own it when the decision exceeds your authority

### Executive (Total Score: 51-55)

**What it looks like:**
- Creates the conditions where the right decision gets made regardless of who makes it
- Restructures incentives, processes, or organizational design to resolve the underlying problem
- Communicates decisions in terms of strategic positioning and organizational capability
- Designs for second and third-order effects
- Creates organizational learning mechanisms, not just one-time decisions

## Common Scoring Errors

1. **Leniency bias for structured responses.** A response that lists "Stakeholders: CEO, VP Sales, Engineers" gets a 2 for Stakeholder Analysis, not a 3. Structure without depth is not depth.

2. **Conflating communication quality with decision quality.** A well-written response with poor reasoning should not score higher than a poorly-written response with excellent reasoning. This rubric measures judgment, not prose.

3. **Awarding points for framework mentions.** Saying "I would use RICE" without actually applying the framework to this specific scenario is not evidence of capability. Mentions without application score at the shallow level.

4. **Penalizing unconventional answers.** A response that makes a counterintuitive decision with strong reasoning should score HIGHER than a conventional decision with weak reasoning. The rubric rewards judgment, not conformity.

5. **Scoring dimensions independently when they should interact.** A strong pre-mortem should connect to reversal conditions. Strong metrics should connect to pre-mortem failure detection. The best responses show coherence across dimensions.

## Using This Rubric for Development

After scoring your response:
1. Identify your two lowest-scoring dimensions.
2. For each, write a specific practice goal (e.g., "I will practice generating at least 4 alternatives for every scenario, including one that changes the constraints").
3. Re-do the same scenario in 2 weeks and compare scores.
4. When you score 4+ on all dimensions for a scenario, move to a more challenging one.
