# Director and VP Product Transition

## The Director Transition: From One Product to Many

The Director of Product transition is the move from owning a product to owning a product organization. Your primary output changes from "great product decisions" to "an organization that makes great product decisions." If you are still the best PM on your team, you are not doing the Director job.

### What Changes

| Dimension | Principal PM | Director |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| **Primary output** | Product strategy and decision quality | Organizational capability and resource allocation |
| **Scope** | One product domain | Multiple products or a product line |
| **Direct reports** | 0-1 (may mentor but not manage) | 3-8 PMs |
| **Time horizon** | 6-18 months | 12-36 months |
| **Key relationship** | Cross-functional peers | VP Product, peer Directors, executive stakeholders |
| **Failure mode** | Wrong strategy for the domain | Wrong resource allocation across domains; weak PM team |

### The Director's Core Responsibilities

**1. Build and develop the PM team.**
- Hiring: you are now responsible for who joins the product organization. A bad hire at Director level costs 12-18 months of productivity and affects everyone the bad hire manages or influences.
- Development: every PM on your team should be better at the end of the year than at the beginning, in ways that are observable and attributable to your coaching.
- Performance: you must make the hard calls on who is performing and who is not. Keeping an underperforming PM on the team damages the product, the team's morale, and the PM's own career more than a difficult transition.
- Calibration: you must ensure that good performance is recognized and rewarded, and that your best PMs have a reason to stay. The cost of losing a great PM is 6-12 months to hire and ramp a replacement, plus the product decisions the great PM would have made.

**2. Allocate resources across products.**
- You control (or influence) how headcount, budget, and leadership attention are allocated across your product portfolio.
- Resource allocation is the Director's primary strategic lever. Strategy that is not reflected in resource allocation is not strategy — it is aspiration.
- The hardest skill: saying no to good ideas from good PMs because the resource needs to go somewhere more important. The Director who funds everything funds nothing.
- Resource allocation must be dynamic. The annual planning cycle is a snapshot, not a commitment. The best Directors reallocate quarterly based on what they have learned.

**3. Maintain cross-product coherence.**
- Your products must work together. Customers experience the whole, not the parts.
- Coherence mechanisms: shared strategy, shared design system, shared platform, shared roadmap review, shared customer feedback loops.
- The Director's job is not to make every product great in isolation. It is to make the portfolio great as a whole. Sometimes that means one product must accept a sub-optimal path to enable the portfolio.

**4. Be the bridge between product teams and company strategy.**
- The company strategy is set at the VP/CPO/CEO level. The products are built by the teams. The Director translates between them.
- Upward: you represent the teams' reality to leadership. "The strategy says we should win the enterprise, but here is what our teams are seeing from enterprise customers."
- Downward: you translate company strategy into context that teams can use to make decisions. "The company is betting on X market. Here is what that means for our product area's priorities, resources, and metrics."

### Common Director Failure Modes

1. **The super-PM.** Continuing to act as the primary PM for the most important product while also "managing" other PMs. You become the bottleneck. Your direct reports do not develop. The product you are personally managing gets attention; everything else gets neglected.
2. **The absentee manager.** Providing so much "autonomy" that PMs get no coaching, no feedback, and no development. Autonomy without support is abandonment.
3. **The resource hoarder.** Accumulating headcount and budget without reallocating to the highest-value opportunities. Your organization grows; its impact does not.
4. **The strategy translator (but not creator).** Translating company strategy downward without adding domain-specific strategic thinking. The Director should add specificity — "here is what the company strategy means for our specific products, customers, and capabilities."
5. **The conflict avoider.** Avoiding hard conversations about performance, resource allocation, or strategic disagreements. Hard conversations avoided today become crises tomorrow.

### The Director's First 90 Days

**Days 1-30: Understand.**
- Meet every PM on your team individually. Ask: What are you working on? Why? What is going well? What is not? What do you need from me?
- Meet every stakeholder (engineering, design, sales, marketing, finance). Ask: What is working in the product organization? What is not? What do you need from product that you are not getting?
- Review the current strategy, roadmap, and metrics. Understand the current state before you change anything.

**Days 31-60: Assess.**
- Who are your strongest PMs? Who needs development? Who needs a different role?
- What is the quality of the current strategy? Is it clear? Is it reflected in resource allocation?
- What are the biggest gaps — strategy, execution, talent, stakeholder relationships, process?
- Do not make changes yet. Observe how the organization operates under normal conditions.

**Days 61-90: Act.**
- Make the one change that will have the biggest impact. Not ten changes — one. The organization can absorb one significant change per quarter.
- Communicate the change clearly: what, why, how it will be measured, what support is available.
- Establish your operating rhythm: how often do you meet with each PM? What is the format of strategy reviews? How do you communicate upward?

## The VP Product Transition: From Products to Portfolio

The VP Product transition is the move from managing a product line to managing the product function. Your primary output is no longer product outcomes — it is the product organization's ability to consistently produce product outcomes. You are responsible for the product strategy, the product organization, and the product culture.

### What Changes

| Dimension | Director | VP Product |
|-----------|----------|------------|
| **Primary output** | Organizational capability within a product line | Product function capability across the company |
| **Scope** | A product line | The entire product portfolio |
| **Direct reports** | 3-8 PMs | Directors, Principal PMs, and sometimes senior ICs |
| **Time horizon** | 12-36 months | 24-60 months |
| **Key relationship** | VP Product, peer Directors | CEO, CPO, C-suite peers, board |
| **Failure mode** | Weak team within the product line | Product strategy disconnected from company strategy; product function not respected |

### The VP Product's Core Responsibilities

**1. Set the product strategy at the company level.**
- The VP Product owns the answer to: what products do we build, for whom, and why will they win?
- This strategy must be clear enough that every PM can use it to make decisions without asking for clarification.
- The VP must make the hard trade-offs that Directors and PMs cannot make because they are optimizing within their domains, not across the portfolio.

**2. Build the product organization.**
- Organizational design: what is the right structure for the product organization given the company's strategy, stage, and culture?
- Hiring and development: who are the product leaders the company needs now and in 2 years? Are we developing them internally or hiring externally?
- Performance culture: what does great product management look like here? How is it recognized, rewarded, and developed?
- Diversity of thought: does the product organization have the breadth of perspectives needed to make good decisions for a diverse customer base?

**3. Be the voice of product in the C-suite.**
- The VP Product must ensure that product considerations are present in company-level decisions: M&A, partnerships, geographic expansion, pricing strategy, organizational design.
- The VP must advocate for the resources (headcount, budget, time) that the product organization needs to succeed, and must demonstrate the return on those resources.
- The VP must manage the relationship with the CEO — the most important relationship in the company for the product function.

**4. Define and steward product culture.**
- How does this organization make product decisions? What is valued? What is not tolerated?
- Product culture is built through: (a) what you celebrate, (b) what you tolerate, (c) what you measure, (d) who you hire and promote, (e) what decisions you make and how you communicate them.

### Common VP Product Failure Modes

1. **The Director-in-a-VP-title.** Operating at Director scope with a VP title. The organization has a VP-shaped hole where strategic direction and organizational stewardship should be.
2. **Strategy by aggregation.** "Product strategy" that is the sum of what each Director wants to do, rather than a coherent set of choices about what the company will and will not build.
3. **The absent product leader.** Spending all time with the CEO, board, and external stakeholders while the product organization operates without direction.
4. **The internal-only product leader.** Spending all time with the product organization while failing to represent product externally and upward. The product function becomes invisible to the company.
5. **The process VP.** Implementing processes and frameworks without improving product outcomes. Process is a means; outcomes are the end. The organization that has great process and bad outcomes has failed.

### The VP-CEO Relationship

The VP-CEO relationship is the foundation of the product function. Without CEO trust and alignment, the VP Product cannot succeed. Key principles:

1. **No surprises.** The CEO should never learn about a product problem from someone else.
2. **Speak the CEO's language.** The CEO cares about growth, competitive position, market share, revenue, and valuation. Frame product decisions in those terms, not in product terms. "This investment will improve our NPS by 10 points" means nothing to most CEOs. "This investment will reduce churn by 2%, which adds $5M to annual recurring revenue" — that the CEO understands.
3. **Bring recommendations, not problems.** The CEO has problems from every function. The VP Product who brings problems without recommendations adds to the CEO's burden. The VP Product who brings problems with well-analyzed recommendations and a clear ask of the CEO is doing their job.
4. **Disagree and commit.** When the CEO makes a product decision you disagree with, you have two choices: (a) make your case, get alignment, or (b) disagree and commit — execute the decision as if it were your own. The third choice — passive resistance — destroys trust and undermines the product organization.
5. **Build trust through small decisions.** Trust is not built in the big moments. It is built in the weekly 1:1s, the quick decisions made well, the problems flagged early, the commitments kept. By the time a big decision arrives, the trust either exists or it does not.

### The VP's Calendar

A VP Product's calendar reveals their actual priorities, whatever their stated priorities are:

- **25% — CEO and board.** Strategy alignment, board presentations, CEO 1:1s, board-level product updates.
- **25% — Product organization.** 1:1s with Directors, product reviews, strategy sessions, hiring and development.
- **20% — Cross-functional leadership.** Engineering, design, sales, marketing, finance — building the relationships that make product decisions stick.
- **15% — Customers and market.** Direct customer contact, market analysis, competitive intelligence. The higher you go, the easier it is to lose customer contact — and the more dangerous that loss becomes.
- **15% — Thinking and synthesis.** Uninterrupted time for strategic thinking, reading, writing, and connecting dots across domains. This is the first thing to be sacrificed when the calendar gets full — and the most important for VP-level impact.

If your calendar does not roughly match this distribution, you are not doing the VP job. You are doing something else with a VP title.
