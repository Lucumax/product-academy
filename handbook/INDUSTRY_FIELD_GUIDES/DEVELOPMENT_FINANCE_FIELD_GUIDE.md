# Development Finance Field Guide

## Quick Scan — Before Any Product Decision

1. **Who is the actual counterparty, and what is their capacity?** Is the counterparty a government ministry, an MDB, a DFI, a private sponsor, or a combination? What is their institutional capacity — can they actually use the product?
2. **What is the development objective, and how will you measure whether the product advances it?** Development finance is not about profit maximization. The product must address additionality, development outcomes, and sustainability.
3. **What is the concessionality structure?** Is the product built on grants, concessional loans, market-rate instruments, or a blend? The funding model shapes everything — pricing, timelines, stakeholder relationships, and success metrics.
4. **What is the preparation-to-implementation timeline?** Development projects take 3-7 years from concept to financial close and another 2-5 years to operation. Does the product deliver value within a stakeholder's tenure?
5. **What could cause this project to fail that is NOT technical?** Political transition, government capacity failure, community opposition, currency crisis, procurement challenge. Development projects fail for non-technical reasons more often than technical ones.

## Regulatory and Policy Triggers

### You involve the World Bank → World Bank Operational Policies and Procurement Framework

World Bank-financed projects must comply with: Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) — 10 standards covering environmental assessment, labor, community health, land acquisition, indigenous peoples, cultural heritage, biodiversity, and stakeholder engagement. Procurement Framework — mandatory procurement methods, eligibility rules, and fraud/corruption provisions. Disbursement guidelines — documentation and reporting requirements.

### You involve private sector → IFC Performance Standards, Equator Principles

IFC Performance Standards define environmental and social requirements for IFC-financed private sector projects. The Equator Principles (adopted by 120+ financial institutions) require applying similar standards to project finance. Key standards: PS1 (Assessment and Management), PS2 (Labor), PS5 (Land Acquisition and Involuntary Resettlement), PS6 (Biodiversity), PS7 (Indigenous Peoples), PS8 (Cultural Heritage).

### You handle government guarantees → IMF/World Bank Debt Sustainability Framework

Government guarantees (for PPPs, sovereign loans, etc.) create contingent liabilities that affect sovereign debt sustainability. The IMF/World Bank DSF assesses whether a country's debt burden is sustainable. Projects that trigger DSF thresholds may face restrictions on new government guarantees.

### You involve climate finance → Paris Agreement alignment, Green Climate Fund requirements

Climate finance must demonstrate alignment with the Paris Agreement (low-carbon and climate-resilient development). GCF-funded projects must meet GCF investment criteria: impact potential, paradigm shift potential, sustainable development potential, country ownership, efficiency and effectiveness. Climate finance tracking (OECD DAC Rio markers, MDB joint methodology) is required for reporting.

### You involve procurement → MDB procurement frameworks, World Bank Procurement Regulations

Procurement financed by MDBs must comply with their procurement frameworks — competitive, transparent, value-for-money based. National procurement systems may be used where they meet international standards. Anti-corruption: MDBs can debar firms for fraud and corruption; debarments are cross-enforced.

## Stakeholder Map

| Stakeholder | What They Care About | Can Stop You? |
|-------------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Borrowing government** | Development impact, political sustainability, fiscal space, sovereignty | Yes — can refuse to approve, delay implementation, or change policy |
| **Ministry of Finance** | Fiscal impact, debt sustainability, contingent liabilities, budget allocation | Yes — controls government guarantees and budget commitments |
| **Line ministry / implementing agency** | Operational functionality, institutional capacity, staff resources | Yes — must execute the project; low capacity = poor execution |
| **MDB / DFI** | Development outcomes, financial sustainability, safeguards compliance, additionality | Yes — controls financing; can suspend or cancel for non-compliance |
| **Donor / climate fund** | Development impact, donor visibility, policy alignment, results reporting | Yes — controls grant funding; can withdraw support |
| **Private sector sponsor** | Financial returns, risk allocation, contractual certainty, exit options | Can withdraw if project economics fail |
| **Commercial lenders** | Credit risk, legal certainty, political risk, security package | Can decline to finance if risk is unacceptable |
| **Civil society / communities** | Environmental and social impacts, benefit sharing, participation, accountability | Can block or delay through opposition, litigation, and political pressure |
| **Evaluation function** | Results achievement, lessons learned, accountability | Can damage institutional reputation and future funding with poor evaluation |
| **Audit / anti-corruption** | Financial integrity, procurement compliance, fraud detection | Can trigger investigation, suspension, debarment |

## Risk Checklist

### Sovereign and political risk
- [ ] Sovereign credit rating and outlook assessed
- [ ] Political stability assessed (upcoming elections, transition risks)
- [ ] Government commitment assessed (is this a priority project?)
- [ ] Policy continuity risk assessed (would a new government support this?)
- [ ] Currency risk modeled (FX mismatch between revenue and debt)
- [ ] Expropriation and breach of contract risk covered (PRI / MIGA / treaty protection)

### Institutional capacity risk
- [ ] Implementing agency capacity assessed (staff, systems, track record)
- [ ] Financial management system adequacy assessed
- [ ] Procurement capacity assessed (can the agency manage competitive procurement?)
- [ ] Monitoring and evaluation capacity assessed
- [ ] Capacity building plan designed (if capacity gap exists)

### Environmental and social risk
- [ ] Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) completed
- [ ] Resettlement Action Plan (RAP) completed (if land acquisition or displacement)
- [ ] Indigenous Peoples Plan completed (if affecting indigenous communities)
- [ ] Stakeholder engagement plan completed (meaningful consultation, not checkbox)
- [ ] Grievance redress mechanism designed and resourced

### Financial risk
- [ ] Project financial model built (base, upside, downside cases)
- [ ] Off-taker / revenue counterparty credit risk assessed
- [ ] Financing plan credible (sources identified, commitments received)
- [ ] Currency mismatch quantified and hedged or mitigated
- [ ] Contingent liabilities on government quantified and within DSF limits
- [ ] Cost overrun risk assessed and contingency budgeted (typically 10-25%)

### Procurement and execution risk
- [ ] Procurement method defined (ICB, NCB, etc.) and plan realistic
- [ ] Construction timeline realistic (accounting for seasonality, logistics, permitting)
- [ ] Supply chain risk assessed (long-lead items, import restrictions, logistics)
- [ ] Contract structure defined (EPC, O&M, etc.) and risk allocation appropriate
- [ ] Force majeure provisions defined

### Development effectiveness risk
- [ ] Results framework / logframe defined with measurable indicators
- [ ] Additionality demonstrated (financial and non-financial)
- [ ] Theory of change articulated (how does the project produce the intended impact?)
- [ ] M&E system designed and baseline data collected
- [ ] Sustainability plan defined (will benefits continue after project closure?)

## Decision Patterns

### Pattern: "This country is high-risk — should we finance this project?"

Risk is not disqualifying. Development finance by definition goes where commercial capital does not. The question is not "is this risky?" but "are the risks identified, priced, and mitigated to the extent possible?" and "does the development benefit justify the residual risk?" A project in a fragile state with high risk and high development impact may be exactly where DFIs should be operating. The key is: (a) risk identification — be honest about what could go wrong, (b) risk mitigation — what can you do about each identified risk, and (c) risk acceptance — for the residual risks, is the board/governance aware and has it accepted the risk?

### Pattern: "The government wants to move faster — can we compress the preparation timeline?"

Compressing preparation means cutting corners on: feasibility studies, ESIA, stakeholder engagement, capacity assessment, procurement planning, risk allocation design. These corners will be rediscovered during implementation as cost overruns, community opposition, procurement failures, and institutional breakdowns. A shorter preparation phase often means a longer implementation phase — and the total project timeline may be the same or longer. "Slow is smooth, smooth is fast" applies.

### Pattern: "The private sector wants a higher return — should we increase the concessionality?"

Use the minimum concessionality necessary. The test: would the private investor proceed with less concessional support? If yes, the concessionality is too high — you are subsidizing private returns at the expense of development resources. Structure the concessionality to crowd in private capital, not subsidize it. The Enhanced Principles on Blended Concessional Finance are the framework.

### Pattern: "This government agency doesn't have capacity — should we set up a PIU?"

Project Implementation Units (PIUs) — externally-funded, externally-staffed project units — are the standard response to low capacity. They work in the short term — the project gets implemented. They fail in the long term — the agency does not develop capacity, the PIU staff leave when the project ends, and the next project needs another PIU. Always have a PIU transition plan: how will the agency absorb the project functions over time?

### Pattern: "Let's build the product for all MDBs — they all do the same thing."

MDBs have different: governance (shareholder structure, voting weight), operational policies, procurement frameworks, safeguard requirements, sector strategies, and country strategies. A product that assumes "MDB" is a category may fail because it misses institution-specific requirements. Build for one MDB first, then adapt.

## Failure Mode Check

| Failure Mode | Early Warning Sign | Mitigation |
|-------------|-------------------|------------|
| Ignoring government capacity | Product assumes institutional processes and staff skills that do not exist | Assess capacity before designing. Simplify to match capacity, include capacity building, or find a higher-capacity starting point. |
| Underestimating political risk | Project assumes stable government policy for the full implementation period | Model political risk scenarios. Contractual protections (stabilization clauses, international arbitration). MDB involvement raises political cost of breach. |
| Optimizing for disbursement | Product success metric is disbursement velocity, not development outcomes | Measure outcomes. A project that disburses fast but produces no results is a failure. Incentives must reward outcomes. |
| Treating all countries as the same | Product works in middle-income countries; fails in fragile states with a "country context problem" label | Segment by institutional capacity. Fragile state product = different feature set, different risk model, different implementation approach. |
| Transaction costs kill small projects | Product works for $200M+ projects; for $10M projects, product-related costs exceed 5% of project value | Tier the product. Light version for small projects with proportionally lower features and lower transaction cost. |
| Technology vs. relationships | Product digitizes processes that depend on personal relationships between country directors and ministers | Understand the social architecture. Technology should support relationships, not attempt to replace them. Automate around relationship workflows. |
| Stakeholder engagement as checkbox | Consultation happened, communities were informed, no objections recorded — but communities oppose the project | Genuine engagement means communities can influence project design. If engagement never changes anything, it is not engagement — it is notification. |

## Key Metrics

### Disbursement metrics (process, not outcome)
- **Disbursement ratio:** Actual disbursement / planned disbursement. Below 80%: project is stalled. Above 120%: project may be rushing (quality risk). Neither extreme is good.
- **Time from approval to first disbursement:** Measures readiness. Target: <3 months for a well-prepared project; >12 months indicates preparation gaps.

### Development outcome metrics (the real metrics)
- **Output achievement:** Physical outputs vs. plan (km of road built, MW installed, people trained).
- **Outcome achievement:** Changes vs. baseline (travel time reduction, electricity access increase, institutional performance improvement).
- **Economic rate of return (ERR):** Net economic benefits relative to costs. Standard threshold: >12% for most infrastructure.
- **Development outcome rating:** IEG/independent evaluation rating (Highly Satisfactory through Highly Unsatisfactory). This is the metric that matters for institutional reputation.

### Financial metrics
- **Financial internal rate of return (FIRR):** Financial returns to the project. Used for commercial viability assessment.
- **Mobilization ratio:** Private capital mobilized / DFI capital committed. Target: >1:3 for blended finance; >1:1 for direct lending.
- **Concession level:** Grant element (present value of concessional benefit / project value). For blended finance: minimum concessionality principle — use the least necessary.

### Institutional metrics
- **Counterparty capacity improvement:** Measured through institutional assessments before, during, and after the project.
- **Policy reform adoption:** Number of policy changes adopted with project support.
- **Safeguard compliance:** Number of non-compliance incidents; time to resolution.

### Project health metrics
- **Project-at-risk flags:** Procurement delays, safeguard incidents, disbursement slowdown, counterparty staffing gaps.
- **Time to prepare:** Months from concept note to board approval. Benchmarked by sector and region.
- **Time to implement:** Months from approval to closing. Extended implementation = increased cost and delayed benefits.

## Language to Use

| Say This | Not This |
|----------|----------|
| "The implementing agency's financial management capacity is rated medium — we need capacity building and interim external FM support." | "The government will handle it." |
| "The project's additionality is demonstrated: the investment would not occur at this scale or timeline without our involvement, based on market sounding." | "This is a good project — it should be financed." |
| "We should use the minimum concessionality necessary to make the senior tranche investable for pension funds." | "Let's write a grant to make the project work." |
| "The ESIA identified community concerns about water access — the project design now includes a community water program." | "The environmental assessment is complete." |
| "The political risk is significant — we need MIGA coverage for expropriation and currency inconvertibility." | "The government has been stable." |
| "We should reserve 15% for cost overruns and 12 months of implementation delay in the project timeline." | "The EPC contractor said they can do it on budget and on time." |
| "The PIU is necessary for Year 1-3, but we need a transition plan that transfers procurement and FM to the agency by Year 4." | "We'll set up a PIU to run the project." |
| "The results framework has 12 indicators — 4 output, 4 outcome, 4 impact — with baseline data collected and targets set." | "We'll track progress." |

|-------------|---------------|------------------|-------------------|
| World Bank (IBRD/IDA) | Sovereign loans (IBRD near-market, IDA concessional) | ESF, Procurement Framework, country partnership framework alignment | Project preparation, procurement, M&E, disbursement platforms |

## Top 5 Things Product Leaders Get Wrong in Development Finance

1. They assume government capacity exists — when the primary constraint on project success is the implementing agency's ability to manage procurement, financial management, and monitoring.
2. They optimize for disbursement speed instead of development outcomes — creating perverse incentives to push projects through preparation before they are ready.
3. They build for large projects ($200M+) and are surprised when the same product is uneconomic for small projects ($10M) — because transaction costs are fixed, not proportional.
4. They treat stakeholder engagement as a regulatory checkbox — when community opposition is the leading cause of project delay, and genuine engagement is the only preventive measure.
5. They build technology to replace relationships in an industry that runs on relationships — and discover that the country director and the finance minister solve problems through trust, not through a platform.
