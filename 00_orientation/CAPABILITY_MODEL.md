# Product Leadership Capability Model

## Why This Model Exists

Most product leadership frameworks are tautological. They define "great PM" as someone who ships features on time, writes clear PRDs, and gets promoted. They mistake correlates for causes — a prestigious MBA, a tour at a FAANG company, fluency with the latest framework, or the ability to produce a polished board deck. None of these are sufficient proof of elite product leadership. Some are actively misleading.

This model defines what product leaders at every level must actually be able to **do** — the capabilities that separate someone who holds a title from someone who creates durable organizational and market outcomes. For every capability, we distinguish level-specific expectations, evidence of real excellence, common false positives that hiring panels and promotion committees routinely confuse with competence, practice methods for deliberate development, and how the capability manifests differently across product archetypes and industries.

The capabilities are organized into four clusters:

1. **Strategic Reasoning** — How you decide what matters
2. **Execution Architecture** — How you convert decisions into outcomes
3. **Organizational Leverage** — How you multiply impact through others
4. **Institutional Leadership** — How you shape the system itself

## Cluster 1: Strategic Reasoning

### 1.1 Problem Selection

The capacity to identify, evaluate, and commit to the right problems — not merely to solve problems well.

**Senior PM expectation:** Selects among problems within a defined product area using structured criteria (impact, feasibility, strategic alignment). Can articulate why a chosen problem matters more than alternatives. Recognizes when a problem is poorly defined and pushes back on vague mandates.

**Principal PM expectation:** Identifies problems the organization didn't know it had. Sees across product boundaries to find highest-leverage intersections. Evaluates problem spaces using multiple lenses (user value, business economics, competitive dynamics, technical debt accrual) and makes tradeoffs explicit. Can convince leadership to abandon a popular initiative in favor of something better.

**Director expectation:** Defines the problem-selection *system* for a product area or portfolio. Establishes evaluation criteria, decision forums, and escalation paths. Ensures teams are solving problems worth solving, not just solving problems well. Intervenes when teams are optimizing locally at the expense of portfolio outcomes.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Defines what problem spaces the company will and will not play in. Makes irreversible commitments visible and contestable. Maintains the distinction between problems the company is structured to solve and problems it wishes it could solve. Knows when a problem-selection mistake requires admitting error versus doubling down.

**Evidence of excellence:** A documented track record of *killing* initiatives that were well-resourced and popular because the problem wasn't worth solving. Teams that routinely pass on good ideas because they have better ones. Product portfolios where the median initiative impact is high, not just the top-decile wins.

**Common false positives:**
- Impressive problem *framing* without evidence of selection discipline
- Solving difficult problems that didn't need solving (the "impressive solution to irrelevant problem" pattern)
- Selecting problems based on exec sponsorship rather than strategic merit
- Maintaining a portfolio without ever saying no to anything

**Practice method:** Maintain a "problem diary" for 90 days. Log every incoming request, idea, and initiative. For each, answer: Why was this selected or rejected? What alternative was foregone? What would have been true that would have reversed the decision? Review with a peer weekly.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform/infrastructure:** Problem selection is dominated by internal customer needs and technical constraints; the hardest skill is distinguishing real platform leverage from premature abstraction
- **Consumer/growth:** Problem selection is heavily influenced by experimentation velocity; the trap is optimizing for measurable marginal gains while missing step-change opportunities
- **Enterprise/SaaS:** Problem selection involves navigating customer commitments, sales pressure, and contract obligations; the hardest skill is saying no to a paying customer's request
- **Marketplace:** Problem selection requires balancing two-sided dynamics; improving one side at the expense of the other is the dominant failure mode
- **AI/ML:** Problem selection must account for model capability uncertainty; the hardest skill is distinguishing "we can't do this yet" from "this will never work"

**Industry variation:**
- **Regulated industries (finance, healthcare):** Problem selection is constrained by compliance and risk frameworks; excellence means finding highest-impact problems within the feasible set
- **Hardware/physical products:** Problem selection has longer feedback cycles and higher cost of being wrong; excellence means front-loading de-risking activities
- **High-growth startups:** Problem selection is dominated by runway and survival constraints; excellence means sequencing problems so that each unlocks the next

### 1.2 Product Thesis

The capacity to articulate a falsifiable, coherent theory of how a product will create durable value — and to update that thesis as evidence accumulates.

**Senior PM expectation:** Can articulate a clear product thesis for their area: who it serves, what problem it solves, why the solution will win, and what key assumptions must hold. Documents the thesis explicitly and revisits it at meaningful milestones.

**Principal PM expectation:** Develops a thesis that spans multiple product areas or customer segments. Identifies the critical path of assumptions and designs the fastest way to validate or invalidate each. Can articulate not just what they believe but what evidence would change their mind.

**Director expectation:** Maintains a coherent portfolio-level thesis. Ensures individual product theses are compatible and collectively addressable. Identifies when an emerging team or initiative has no thesis (just a roadmap) and intervenes. Can defend the thesis to skeptical executives and board members.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Owns the company-level product thesis. Ensures the thesis is legible to the entire organization — not just the product team. Updates the thesis in response to market shifts, competitive moves, and internal learning. Knows when to hold the thesis against short-term pressure and when to abandon it.

**Evidence of excellence:** The thesis makes specific, falsifiable predictions that turn out to be correct. When the thesis is wrong, the leader identifies and communicates this before the market forces the revelation. Team members at all levels can articulate the thesis without reference to slides.

**Common false positives:**
- A compelling vision statement that is not falsifiable
- A thesis that explains everything post-hoc but makes no prospective predictions
- Confusing a product strategy document with a product thesis
- A thesis that no one in the organization can reconstruct without the leader present

**Practice method:** Write a one-page product thesis that includes: (1) the core insight about customer behavior or market structure, (2) three specific, dated predictions that follow from the insight, (3) the evidence that would cause you to abandon each prediction. Share with someone who will hold you accountable. Revisit quarterly.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform/infrastructure:** The thesis must account for adoption curves across internal teams and the risk of building capabilities no team is ready to consume
- **Consumer/growth:** The thesis is often centered on a behavioral insight about user psychology or a distribution advantage
- **Enterprise/SaaS:** The thesis must account for buyer/user divergence and organizational change management
- **AI/ML:** The thesis must explicitly address model improvement trajectories and data flywheel assumptions

**Industry variation:**
- **Deep tech:** The thesis is inseparable from technical feasibility assumptions; the hardest skill is distinguishing scientific risk from execution risk
- **Marketplaces:** Network effects are central to the thesis; the hardest skill is distinguishing real network effects from wishful thinking
- **Subscription businesses:** The thesis must account for retention economics not just acquisition

### 1.3 Customer and Domain Understanding

The capacity to develop deep, non-obvious understanding of customers and their context — going beyond surface-level needs to structural, behavioral, and systemic insight.

**Senior PM expectation:** Maintains direct, regular customer contact. Can describe customer workflows, pain points, and decision-making processes with precision. Distinguishes between what customers say and what they do. Can map the customer's organizational and economic context.

**Principal PM expectation:** Has domain expertise deep enough to challenge industry assumptions. Can predict how customers will respond to changes before testing. Identifies customer needs that customers themselves cannot articulate. Develops mental models of customer behavior that generalize across segments.

**Director expectation:** Ensures the organization maintains sufficient customer context to make good decisions. Identifies when teams are building from stale or superficial understanding. Creates systems (not just exhortations) that force regular, structured customer exposure. Can represent the customer perspective in executive forums without distortion.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Knows the customer and domain well enough to make high-stakes bets without exhaustive research. Maintains customer relationships at senior levels that provide unfiltered strategic signal. Ensures the organization's customer understanding isn't dependent on any single individual.

**Evidence of excellence:** The leader makes accurate predictions about customer behavior that surprise industry insiders. Teams report that customer insights from this leader changed their direction. Customers say that this leader understands their business better than they do.

**Common false positives:**
- Attending many customer meetings without developing deep models
- Reciting customer anecdotes without statistical or structural understanding
- Confusing domain *familiarity* (years in the industry) with domain *insight*
- User research reports that are voluminous but induce no decisions

**Practice method:** For your most important customer segment, answer: (1) What is their economic model — how do they make and lose money? (2) What organizational dysfunction prevents them from adopting a better solution? (3) What would have to be true about their world for our product to be irrelevant? Validate answers with at least five direct customer conversations.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform/infrastructure:** The "customer" is internal teams; domain understanding requires deep technical empathy and understanding of internal developer workflows
- **Enterprise/SaaS:** Domain understanding must span the buyer (economic buyer), the user (daily user), and the administrator (deployment and management)
- **Consumer:** Domain understanding requires behavioral psychology sophistication and comfort with quantitative behavioral data at scale
- **Healthcare:** Domain understanding requires navigating the patient-provider-payer triangle and regulatory constraints

**Industry variation:**
- **B2B vertical SaaS:** Industry-specific workflow expertise is non-negotiable; generalist PM skills are insufficient
- **Horizontal platforms:** Domain understanding must span multiple industries; the skill is pattern recognition across domains
- **Emerging categories:** There is no established domain; the skill is sensemaking under ambiguity

### 1.4 Product Judgment and Taste

The capacity to make sound product decisions in the absence of complete data — decisions about scope, quality, interaction design, and the intangible qualities that distinguish great products from adequate ones.

**Senior PM expectation:** Makes scope and design decisions that are internally consistent and defensible. Can articulate the principles behind their decisions. Knows when to escalate a judgment call to more senior leaders. Ships products that are coherent if not yet distinctive.

**Principal PM expectation:** Has developed product judgment that others seek out. Makes decisions that improve a product's coherence, simplicity, and distinctiveness. Can identify the one or two details that will most shape user perception. Knows when a product is "not good enough yet" without being able to fully articulate why — and is usually right.

**Director expectation:** Sets and enforces quality standards across a portfolio. Can evaluate products they didn't build and identify weaknesses the team missed. Develops taste in others — not by dictating decisions but by teaching frameworks, providing honest feedback, and modeling good judgment. Prevents the organization from shipping products that would damage reputation or trust.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Represents the organization's product taste externally. Makes final calls on divisive product decisions. Ensures product quality is not sacrificed to schedule pressure, political convenience, or feature-count metrics. Maintains taste consistency across a growing, diversifying organization.

**Evidence of excellence:** Products that feel coherent even under constraints. Teams that make better decisions when the leader is absent than competing teams make with their leader present. External recognition of product quality from users and critics, not just internal stakeholders.

**Common false positives:**
- Strong aesthetic opinions that don't connect to user outcomes
- Micromanaging design decisions rather than developing taste in others
- Shipping polished products that solve the wrong problem
- Taste that only works in one domain or archetype
- Using "taste" as a justification for personal preference without evidence

**Practice method:** For every significant product decision you make, write a brief "decision record" that captures: what you decided, what alternatives you considered, what principles you applied, and what you'd need to observe to conclude you were wrong. Review these records quarterly with someone whose judgment you respect.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Consumer:** Visual and interaction polish matter disproportionately; micro-interactions define perception
- **Developer tools:** Taste manifests as API design elegance, documentation quality, and consistency of mental models
- **Enterprise:** Taste often involves knowing what to leave complex or customizable versus what to simplify
- **AI/ML:** Taste involves judgments about model behavior, safety guardrails, and how to handle failure modes gracefully

**Industry variation:**
- **Gaming:** Taste is central — it's an entertainment product; product judgment is inseparable from creative judgment
- **Fintech:** Trust and perceived security are dimensions of taste that override aesthetics
- **Healthcare:** Safety and clinical validity are non-negotiable dimensions of product judgment

### 1.5 Technical Fluency

The capacity to engage productively with technical systems, constraints, and decisions — not necessarily to code, but to reason effectively about technical tradeoffs.

**Senior PM expectation:** Understands the technical architecture of their product area. Can discuss tradeoffs with engineers using accurate mental models. Knows when to ask for technical deep-dives. Can roughly estimate technical complexity. Understands the difference between a technical constraint and a technical preference.

**Principal PM expectation:** Can contribute to technical strategy discussions, not just observe them. Understands system-level implications of local technical decisions. Can identify when a technical proposal is elegant but strategically wrong. Bridges product and engineering perspectives without being captured by either.

**Director expectation:** Evaluates technical proposals from multiple teams for coherence and conflict. Identifies technical decisions that create organizational lock-in. Ensures product strategy is technically informed without being technically determined. Can represent technical tradeoffs to non-technical executives.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Engages credibly on architecture decisions with the CTO. Understands the technical debt portfolio and its strategic implications. Can evaluate the technical dimension of M&A targets or build-vs-buy decisions. Maintains enough technical depth to detect when the organization is being BS'd on feasibility.

**Evidence of excellence:** Engineers seek out the leader's perspective on technical tradeoffs. The leader accurately predicts which technical investments will pay off and which will create future constraints. Technical surprises in the leader's area are rare and genuinely unforeseeable.

**Common false positives:**
- Having been an engineer previously (past technical skill ≠ current technical fluency in a different stack)
- Using technical vocabulary without understanding the underlying concepts
- Deferring entirely to engineering on all technical decisions
- Over-indexing on technical considerations at the expense of user and business context

**Practice method:** Pair with a senior engineer to trace a production incident from symptom to root cause. For your product's three most important technical systems, draw their architecture from memory and have an engineer correct it. Read one engineering design document per month from a team outside your area.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform/infrastructure:** Technical fluency requirements are highest; must understand distributed systems, API design, scalability constraints
- **AI/ML:** Must understand model evaluation, data pipelines, prompt engineering concepts, and the difference between model capabilities and product capabilities
- **Consumer mobile:** Must understand mobile platform constraints, performance budgets, and offline/online synchronization patterns
- **Enterprise:** Must understand integration patterns, authentication/authorization, data residency, and security models

**Industry variation:**
- **Hardware:** Must understand manufacturing constraints, supply chain implications, and the irreversibility of hardware decisions
- **Fintech:** Must understand transaction processing, reconciliation, and regulatory technical requirements
- **Healthcare:** Must understand interoperability standards (HL7, FHIR), data privacy architectures, and clinical workflow integration

### 1.6 Data and Evaluation Fluency

The capacity to define meaningful metrics, design evaluation approaches, interpret data correctly, and avoid the most common analytical errors.

**Senior PM expectation:** Defines metrics that measure outcomes, not just output. Designs experiments with statistical validity. Distinguishes correlation from causation in product data. Knows the limitations of their data and metrics. Communicates findings clearly, including uncertainty.

**Principal PM expectation:** Designs measurement systems, not just individual metrics. Identifies when the organization is optimizing a metric that has become decoupled from value. Can design evaluation approaches for hard-to-measure outcomes (trust, quality, developer productivity). Detects and corrects for survivorship bias, selection effects, and Simpson's paradox.

**Director expectation:** Ensures the organization's metrics create alignment rather than dysfunction. Identifies when teams are gaming metrics. Establishes evaluation standards that are appropriate to the decision stakes. Can explain to executives why a metric that looks good is misleading.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Defines the company-level measurement philosophy. Determines which metrics go to the board and why. Ensures the organization can distinguish signal from noise at the portfolio level. Prevents metric fixation from driving out judgment.

**Evidence of excellence:** Metrics that are stable predictors of long-term outcomes. Teams that abandon initiatives early because the data says they should. An organization that uses data to inform decisions without becoming paralyzed without it. No major surprises that "the data should have told us."

**Common false positives:**
- Dashboard proliferation without decision impact
- Statistical sophistication applied to the wrong questions
- Confusing measurement precision with measurement accuracy
- Citing data to justify decisions already made for other reasons
- A/B testing everything, including things that shouldn't be A/B tested

**Practice method:** For your top three metrics, trace them end-to-end: how are they logged? What edge cases are excluded? What does movement in the metric actually imply about user behavior? Find one product decision your team made this quarter that would have been different with better measurement.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Consumer/growth:** Heavy emphasis on experimentation, causal inference, and behavioral metrics; risk of optimizing for measurable short-term metrics at expense of long-term value
- **Enterprise/SaaS:** Metrics must span acquisition, adoption, retention, expansion; product usage data is often incomplete or lagging
- **Platform:** Metrics involve internal adoption, reliability, and developer productivity — all notoriously hard to measure well
- **AI/ML:** Evaluation requires offline metrics, online metrics, and human evaluation; distribution shift and feedback loops are dominant risks

**Industry variation:**
- **Advertising:** Metrics are abundant but adversarial (fraud, gaming); sophisticated measurement is table stakes
- **Healthcare:** Outcome measurement is intrinsically difficult — long time horizons, confounding factors, ethical constraints on experimentation
- **Education:** Learning outcomes are the ultimate metric but are hard to measure and slow to manifest

### 1.7 Business Economics

The capacity to understand and influence the economic model of the product and the business — unit economics, pricing, monetization, cost structures, and their strategic implications.

**Senior PM expectation:** Understands the unit economics of their product area. Can model how product changes affect revenue, cost, and margin. Makes product decisions that are economically informed. Can explain the business model to anyone on the team.

**Principal PM expectation:** Identifies product opportunities that change the economic model, not just optimize within it. Can build economic models that guide portfolio decisions. Understands the relationship between product decisions, pricing strategy, and competitive dynamics. Quantifies the economic value delivered to customers.

**Director expectation:** Owns the economic performance of a product line or portfolio. Makes investment and disinvestment decisions based on economic analysis. Identifies when the unit economics don't support the strategy. Can present P&L implications to finance and executive audiences.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Defines the economic strategy for the product organization. Determines pricing and packaging strategy. Allocates capital across product investments. Can defend the product investment thesis to the board in financial terms. Understands how the company's economic model compares to competitors and substitutes.

**Evidence of excellence:** Product decisions that observably improve business economics, not just user metrics. Pricing changes that increase both revenue and customer satisfaction. Investment allocation that demonstrates understanding of diminishing returns and opportunity cost.

**Common false positives:**
- Financial modeling skill without strategic judgment about what to model
- Confusing revenue growth with healthy unit economics
- Optimizing for metrics that are rewarded by compensation structures rather than true business value
- Deferring all economic thinking to finance/strategy teams

**Practice method:** Build a unit economic model for your product area from scratch. Identify the three assumptions that most affect the outcome. For each assumption, determine how you could validate it within 30 days. Present the model to someone in finance for critique.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Marketplace:** Economics involve both sides; take rate, liquidity, and subsidy decisions are central
- **SaaS:** Subscription economics (CAC, LTV, churn, expansion revenue) dominate; pricing and packaging are strategic weapons
- **Consumer:** Monetization may be indirect (ads, data); attention economics and engagement depth matter more than direct revenue
- **Platform:** Economics involve build-vs-buy, make-vs-partner, and the cost of internal versus external customers

**Industry variation:**
- **Fintech:** Unit economics are heavily shaped by regulatory capital requirements, fraud costs, and transaction processing fees
- **Healthcare:** Reimbursement models (fee-for-service, value-based care, capitation) fundamentally shape product economics
- **Hardware:** Gross margin constraints, inventory costs, and supply chain economics dominate

### 1.8 Risk Judgment

The capacity to identify, assess, and make decisions under conditions of uncertainty — distinguishing risks worth taking from risks worth mitigating.

**Senior PM expectation:** Identifies risks in their product area (technical, user, business, legal, reputational). Communicates risks clearly and proportionally. Distinguishes between risks they can mitigate and risks that require escalation. Doesn't let risk awareness become risk paralysis.

**Principal PM expectation:** Evaluates risk across multiple dimensions simultaneously. Identifies correlated risks that look independent but aren't. Makes risk-return tradeoffs explicit — not just "this is risky" but "this risk is worth taking because..." Knows when a risk is acceptably small and when it demands attention.

**Director expectation:** Manages the risk portfolio across a product area — ensuring aggregate risk is understood, not just each individual risk. Prevents teams from over-mitigating small risks while ignoring large ones. Can communicate risk posture to executives without causing panic or complacency.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Defines the organization's risk appetite. Makes the biggest risk decisions — those that could materially affect the company. Ensures the board understands the product risk portfolio. Maintains organizational calm during realized risks (incidents, failures, bets that don't pay off).

**Evidence of excellence:** A portfolio where risks are taken deliberately and the organization learns from both good and bad outcomes. No "how did we not see this coming" moments on major risks. Teams that feel safe raising risks without being punished for being "negative."

**Common false positives:**
- Risk identification without risk mitigation or risk acceptance decisions
- Treating all risks as equally important
- Risk aversion disguised as prudence
- Risk-taking disguised as boldness without explicit acknowledgment of what's being risked
- After-the-fact explanations for why a realized risk "couldn't have been predicted"

**Practice method:** For your three most significant product decisions in the past quarter, write a brief pre-mortem: assume the decision failed badly. What caused the failure? What signals were available that you discounted? What would you do differently?

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **AI/ML:** Model safety, bias, and reliability risks are novel and poorly understood; regulatory risk is evolving rapidly
- **Fintech:** Fraud, compliance, and systemic risk are existential; the cost of being wrong can exceed the value of being right
- **Healthcare:** Patient safety risks are non-negotiable; clinical risk assessment requires domain expertise
- **Platform:** Breaking changes create cascading risks across dependent systems; blast radius assessment is essential

**Industry variation:**
- **Defense/government:** Security classification, export controls, and mission-critical reliability create unique risk dimensions
- **Social media:** Content moderation, platform responsibility, and societal impact risks are distinct and evolving
- **Crypto/web3:** Smart contract risk, custody risk, and regulatory uncertainty dominate

### 1.9 Portfolio Allocation

The capacity to allocate finite resources (people, time, attention, capital) across competing opportunities to maximize outcomes.

**Senior PM expectation:** Allocates their own time and attention effectively across competing demands. Makes roadmap tradeoffs within their area that reflect strategic priorities. Can explain why they're working on X rather than Y to anyone who asks.

**Principal PM expectation:** Allocates resources across multiple initiatives or teams. Identifies when an initiative should receive more or less investment based on emerging evidence. Makes allocation decisions that optimize at the portfolio level, not locally.

**Director expectation:** Owns the allocation of headcount, budget, and leadership attention across a product area. Makes explicit investment theses for each initiative. Reallocates resources as evidence accumulates — not just allocating once during annual planning. Resists the gravitational pull of maintaining status quo allocations.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Allocates the company's total product investment across the portfolio. Determines the right balance between core business, adjacent expansion, and transformational bets. Makes allocation decisions that are legible to the board. Knows when to concentrate resources and when to diversify. Explicitly manages the exploration-exploitation tradeoff.

**Evidence of excellence:** Resources flow to the highest-opportunity initiatives regardless of sunk costs, organizational momentum, or executive sponsorship. Teams understand why they're resourced at their current level. Allocation changes are made based on evidence, not just calendar-driven planning cycles. The portfolio contains an appropriate mix of horizons.

**Common false positives:**
- Spreadsheet-driven allocation that ignores qualitative strategic judgment
- Equal allocation across initiatives (false fairness)
- Allocation driven by organizational politics rather than opportunity
- Maintaining allocation to failing initiatives to avoid admitting mistakes
- Allocation that never changes between planning cycles

**Practice method:** Map your current allocation of resources (time, headcount, budget) across initiatives. For each, answer: If we stopped this today, what would we lose? If we doubled investment in it, what would we gain? Identify the initiative where a 10% reallocation would produce the highest return.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform:** Must balance feature work against infrastructure investment; the payoff of infrastructure is delayed and diffuse
- **Consumer:** Must balance growth investments against monetization; the optimal mix changes with scale
- **Enterprise:** Must balance new customer acquisition against existing customer retention and expansion
- **Marketplace:** Must balance supply-side and demand-side investments; the optimal mix changes with marketplace maturity

**Industry variation:**
- **Venture-backed startups:** Runway constraints dominate; allocation must account for funding milestones
- **Public companies:** Quarterly earnings pressure creates bias toward short-term allocation; resisting this is a leadership skill
- **Bootstrapped companies:** Profitability constraints dominate; allocation must be self-funding

## Cluster 2: Execution Architecture

### 2.1 Strategic Sequencing

The capacity to order initiatives and capabilities over time to maximize cumulative outcomes — recognizing that *when* you do something is often as important as *what* you do.

**Senior PM expectation:** Sequences work within a quarter or release to manage dependencies and deliver value incrementally. Can articulate the logic of the sequence. Adjusts sequence when assumptions prove wrong.

**Principal PM expectation:** Sequences initiatives across multiple quarters to create compounding effects. Identifies capabilities that unlock future opportunities. Designs sequences that produce learning at the lowest possible cost. Recognizes when the right next step is counterintuitive.

**Director expectation:** Sequences across a multi-year horizon for a product area. Ensures sequences across teams are compatible. Identifies when the organization should parallelize versus when it should serialize. Balances sequencing for speed against sequencing for learning.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Owns the multi-year strategic sequence for the entire product portfolio. Determines the company's "next best step" in the context of market timing, competitive dynamics, and organizational capacity. Makes sequencing decisions that account for the organization's ability to absorb change.

**Evidence of excellence:** The organization repeatedly finds that initiative B is dramatically easier because initiative A was done first — and initiative A was chosen partly for that reason. Competitors who attempt to skip ahead in the sequence struggle. The sequence produces compounding advantage, not just a series of independent wins.

**Common false positives:**
- A roadmap organized by date rather than by dependency logic
- Sequencing that optimizes for engineering convenience rather than strategic accumulation
- "Big bang" approaches that try to do everything at once because the leader couldn't make sequencing decisions
- Sequencing that ignores organizational learning capacity (doing too much too fast)

**Practice method:** For your current roadmap, draw the dependency graph between initiatives — not just technical dependencies but capability dependencies, learning dependencies, and market dependencies. Identify the one initiative that, if done first, would make everything else easier or faster. Identify the initiative that could be delayed 12 months with minimal consequence.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform:** Sequencing must account for adoption maturity; building platform capabilities before teams are ready to consume them is wasted investment
- **AI/ML:** Sequencing must account for data accumulation and model improvement trajectories; data flywheels require patience
- **Enterprise:** Sequencing must account for customer adoption capacity; enterprise customers can only absorb so much change
- **Marketplace:** Sequencing must account for liquidity thresholds; premature scaling on either side is destructive

**Industry variation:**
- **Regulated industries:** Regulatory approvals create hard sequencing gates that must be planned around
- **Hardware:** Manufacturing lead times and tooling investments create irreversible sequencing commitments
- **Venture-backed:** Funding milestones create sequencing constraints; the sequence must produce fundable evidence at the right moments

### 2.2 Cross-Functional Influence

The capacity to align people across functions — engineering, design, marketing, sales, finance, legal, operations — around product decisions without formal authority over them.

**Senior PM expectation:** Influences engineering and design partners through clear rationale, shared context, and demonstrated respect for their expertise. Builds relationships that survive disagreements. Can navigate a disagreement with an engineering lead without escalating.

**Principal PM expectation:** Influences across organizational boundaries — not just their immediate team. Builds coalitions for complex, cross-functional initiatives. Identifies when influence requires bringing in additional stakeholders or changing the decision frame. Can influence senior leaders in other functions.

**Director expectation:** Creates the conditions for influence at scale — ensuring PMs have the relationships, context, and skills to influence effectively. Intervenes when cross-functional dynamics are blocking progress. Models effective influence in the most challenging situations. Can influence peers at the VP level in other functions.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Influences the executive team and board on product strategy and investment. Maintains productive relationships with functional leaders (CEO, CTO, CRO, CFO, CMO) even during disagreement. Represents product perspective in company-defining decisions (M&A, restructurings, strategy shifts). Builds external influence with partners, analysts, and the market.

**Evidence of excellence:** Cross-functional partners say they make better decisions because of the product leader's involvement. Disagreements are resolved productively without escalation becoming the norm. Functions voluntarily align with product direction because they believe in it, not because they're compelled to.

**Common false positives:**
- Charisma or likability without substantive influence on decisions
- Being included in meetings without actually shaping outcomes
- Influence through escalation (getting a senior sponsor to mandate compliance)
- Confusing being "data-driven" with being influential (data rarely speaks for itself)
- Avoiding conflict rather than working through it productively

**Practice method:** Map your key cross-functional relationships. For each, rate: Do they seek your input before making decisions that affect your product? Do you seek theirs? When you disagree, do you reach a better decision than either of you would have alone? Identify the relationship where your influence is weakest and design a specific approach to improve it.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Enterprise:** Sales and customer success influence is critical; PMs who can't partner with sales will be bypassed
- **Platform:** Engineering influence is paramount; PMs who can't earn engineering respect will be irrelevant
- **Consumer:** Marketing and brand influence is critical; PM must influence how the product is positioned and messaged
- **AI/ML:** Research and data science influence requires distinct approaches; research timelines and product timelines often conflict

**Industry variation:**
- **Highly regulated:** Legal and compliance influence is non-negotiable; PMs must build productive relationships with functions that have veto power
- **Hardware:** Supply chain and manufacturing influence is critical; product decisions have factory-floor implications
- **Professional services companies:** Services delivery influence is critical; product must influence how services are sold and delivered

### 2.3 Executive Communication

The capacity to communicate product strategy, decisions, and results to executive audiences — adapting substance, format, and cadence to the needs of the audience.

**Senior PM expectation:** Communicates clearly and concisely to their direct leadership chain. Can present a product update without relying on dense slide decks. Answers executive questions directly rather than deflecting. Knows when to bring their manager into executive communications.

**Principal PM expectation:** Communicates directly to VP-level executives on complex topics. Anticipates executive questions and addresses them proactively. Can distill a complex product decision into a one-page memo that an executive can consume in five minutes. Handles adversarial questioning without becoming defensive.

**Director expectation:** Communicates to C-suite executives regularly. Structures communications for executive decision-making — not just informing. Can represent product performance to the board when asked. Manages executive expectations proactively, not reactively. Ensures their PMs are developing executive communication skills.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Communicates to the board, investors, analysts, and the market. Crafts the product narrative that shapes external perception. Manages board-level product discussions — ensuring the board has the right information, at the right altitude, at the right time. Builds board confidence in the product organization's judgment.

**Evidence of excellence:** Executives make decisions based on the leader's communications without requiring multiple clarification rounds. Board members reference the product strategy accurately in external conversations. Bad news is communicated early and clearly without triggering panic. Good news is communicated without hype.

**Common false positives:**
- Polished presentation skills without substantive clarity
- Communications that sound impressive but contain no falsifiable claims
- Telling executives what they want to hear rather than what they need to hear
- Overwhelming executives with data rather than providing a clear point of view
- Confusing frequent communication with effective communication

**Practice method:** For your next executive communication, write the one-paragraph version first. What's the single most important thing the executive needs to know? What decision do you need from them? What's the risk they're being asked to accept? Then build supporting material only as needed.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform:** Executive communication must translate technical concepts into business impact without losing essential nuance
- **AI/ML:** Executive communication must manage expectations about AI capabilities while conveying genuine opportunity
- **Enterprise:** Executive communication must bridge product and revenue narratives credibly
- **Consumer:** Executive communication must convey product judgment and taste, not just metrics

**Industry variation:**
- **Public companies:** Earnings call implications shape executive communication; materiality standards apply
- **Venture-backed:** Fundraising narrative shapes executive communication; growth story must be credible
- **PE-backed:** Cash flow and operational metrics dominate; executive communication is financially oriented

---

### 2.4 Outcome Ownership

The capacity to hold oneself and one's organization accountable for outcomes — not just output, not just effort, but actual results in the world.

**Senior PM expectation:** Defines clear, measurable outcomes for their product area. Reports honestly on progress, including when things aren't working. Distinguishes between output (features shipped) and outcomes (problems solved, value created). Feels personal accountability for results.

**Principal PM expectation:** Owns outcomes that span multiple teams or product areas. When outcomes aren't being achieved, investigates honestly rather than deflecting blame. Makes outcome accountability visible and concrete. Designs product work so that outcome evaluation is possible (not everything is a "strategic bet" with a 3-year horizon).

**Director expectation:** Creates an organizational culture of outcome accountability. Ensures teams have both the autonomy and the measurement infrastructure to be accountable. Holds teams accountable without micromanaging. When outcomes aren't achieved, distinguishes between execution failure, strategy failure, and measurement failure.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Bears ultimate accountability for product outcomes to the board, investors, and market. Defines what "good" looks like at the portfolio level. Maintains outcome accountability over multi-year horizons where annual metrics can mislead. Absorbs organizational pressure to redefine failure as success.

**Evidence of excellence:** The organization reliably achieves the outcomes it commits to. When it doesn't, the explanation is specific and credible. Teams don't hide bad news or redefine metrics to make themselves look good. Outcome misses lead to learning and adjustment, not blame and cover-up.

**Common false positives:**
- Setting outcomes so vague or distant that "accountability" is meaningless
- Claiming credit for outcomes that would have happened anyway
- Redefining metrics mid-cycle to convert failures into successes
- Holding others accountable for outcomes without providing the authority and resources to achieve them
- Confusing activity ("we did 47 customer interviews") with outcomes

**Practice method:** For your top three outcome commitments this quarter, write the specific, measurable criteria that will determine success or failure at quarter end. Share these with your manager and team. At quarter end, evaluate honestly and publicly. Note: this is uncomfortable. That's the point.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform:** Outcome measurement is inherently indirect (platform success manifests through consumer success); requires sophisticated proxy metrics
- **Enterprise:** Revenue outcomes are clear but product-outcome attribution is contested with sales and marketing
- **Consumer:** Engagement outcomes are measurable but can be gamed; long-term outcomes (habit formation, brand loyalty) are harder
- **AI/ML:** Outcome measurement must distinguish model quality from product quality from business impact

**Industry variation:**
- **Healthcare:** Clinical outcomes are the ultimate measure but are slow and contested; intermediate outcomes are necessary but potentially misleading
- **Education:** Learning outcomes are the ultimate measure; engagement is a common false proxy
- **Government:** Public value outcomes are hard to measure; process compliance often substitutes for outcome accountability

## Cluster 3: Organizational Leverage

### 3.1 Adoption and Change Management

The capacity to ensure that products are actually adopted by users and that the organizational changes required for adoption occur — recognizing that building a product and getting it used are distinct challenges.

**Senior PM expectation:** Plans for adoption as part of product development, not as an afterthought. Identifies adoption barriers and designs mitigations. Works with go-to-market functions to ensure launch readiness. Measures adoption and iterates based on data.

**Principal PM expectation:** Designs products for adoptability, not just usability. Identifies organizational, behavioral, and psychological barriers to adoption. Develops adoption strategies for products that require behavior change or organizational change. Can lead adoption efforts that span multiple customer segments.

**Director expectation:** Ensures the organization has adoption capability — not just product development capability. Identifies when a product failed not because it was bad but because adoption was neglected. Builds organizational muscle around launch planning, change management, and adoption measurement.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Ensures the product organization is accountable for adoption outcomes, not just delivery outcomes. Defines the relationship between product and go-to-market functions. Personally leads major change management efforts with key customers or partners. Represents the product in sales conversations for strategic deals.

**Evidence of excellence:** Products that achieve target adoption rates without requiring heroic, unsustainable efforts from sales or customer success. Adoption forecasts that are accurate. Features that are adopted because the change management was designed in, not bolted on.

**Common false positives:**
- Launching products and declaring victory while adoption languishes
- Blaming sales/marketing for adoption failures without examining product-adoptability
- Treating adoption as someone else's job
- Designing for ideal users who don't need change management rather than real users who do

**Practice method:** For your last major launch, trace the adoption funnel from awareness through habitual use. Where is the biggest drop-off? What would you change in the product (not the marketing) to improve it? Interview three users who didn't adopt and ask why.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Enterprise:** Change management is central — enterprise adoption requires organizational change; training, migration tools, and administrator experience are product features
- **Platform:** Internal adoption requires developer relations, documentation, and migration support; adoption is a product discipline
- **Consumer:** Adoption is driven by onboarding experience, network effects, and habit formation mechanics
- **AI/ML:** Adoption requires trust-building, expectation management, and integration into existing workflows

**Industry variation:**
- **Healthcare:** Adoption requires clinical workflow integration, training, and often regulatory approval; change management is measured in months or years
- **Education:** Adoption requires teacher training, curriculum integration, and institutional buy-in
- **Government:** Adoption requires procurement processes, security reviews, and stakeholder management

---

### 3.2 Organizational Multiplication

The capacity to increase the organization's productive output without proportionally increasing its size — through systems, culture, standards, and capability development.

**Senior PM expectation:** Makes their immediate team more effective through clear communication, good process, and removing obstacles. Documents decisions and context so the team can operate without constant re-explanation.

**Principal PM expectation:** Multiplies effectiveness across multiple teams. Creates frameworks, templates, and decision tools that other PMs adopt. Mentors PMs outside their direct reporting chain. Identifies organizational friction and proposes solutions. Elevates the organization's standards through example.

**Director expectation:** Designs the operating system for a product organization — decision rights, planning cadences, review forums, information flows. Ensures the organization gets more capable over time, not just larger. Identifies when organizational process is creating overhead without improving decisions and simplifies.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Multiplies product leadership capability across the entire organization. Ensures the product culture persists as the organization scales, diversifies, and changes. Builds systems that develop future product leaders. Creates an organization that is better at product development tomorrow than it was yesterday.

**Evidence of excellence:** The organization's output grows faster than its headcount. PMs who joined two years ago are recognizably more capable than they were. The organization doesn't slow down as it grows. Good ideas spread without the leader personally transmitting them.

**Common false positives:**
- Creating process for its own sake
- Mandating frameworks that teams adopt superficially
- Confusing documentation with capability development
- Building a larger organization as a proxy for multiplying effectiveness
- Taking credit for others' growth that would have happened anyway

**Practice method:** Identify the one thing that, if every PM in your organization did it well, would most increase organizational effectiveness. Now design a system — not a training session — that would make that behavior the default.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- Variegated organizations with multiple product archetypes require meta-frameworks that work across archetypes, not one-size-fits-all processes.
- The multiplication challenge is harder in organizations where PMs work on fundamentally different types of products.

**Industry variation:**
- **High-growth companies:** Multiplication must happen while the organization is doubling every year — systems must be lightweight and self-reinforcing
- **Mature companies:** Multiplication often requires removing legacy process before adding new capability
- **Acquisition-heavy companies:** Multiplication must work across acquired teams with different cultures and practices

---

### 3.3 Talent Development

The capacity to assess, develop, deploy, and retain product talent — building an organization that attracts and grows great product people.

**Senior PM expectation:** Provides honest, useful feedback to peers. Contributes to interviewing and hiring. May mentor more junior PMs. Models good product practice.

**Principal PM expectation:** Develops PMs on their team and adjacent teams through coaching, feedback, and stretch assignments. Contributes meaningfully to hiring decisions. Identifies high-potential PMs and creates opportunities for them. Can have hard conversations about performance.

**Director expectation:** Owns talent development for a product organization. Builds a bench of PM talent. Makes effective hiring, promotion, and (when necessary) exit decisions. Creates development paths that work for different PM archetypes. Ensures performance management is honest and developmental, not bureaucratic.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Defines the talent strategy for the product organization. Determines the mix of internal development and external hiring. Builds executive-level product talent. Creates a product culture that attracts great PMs. Ensures diversity of background, thinking, and approach in the product organization.

**Evidence of excellence:** PMs who leave the organization are sought after. PMs who join grow measurably. The organization produces a disproportionate share of industry product leaders. Promotion decisions are widely regarded as fair and accurate. Performance issues are addressed, not avoided.

**Common false positives:**
- Hiring from prestigious companies without evaluating actual capability
- Promoting based on visibility rather than demonstrated capability
- Retaining underperformers to avoid difficult conversations
- Confusing tenure with development
- Talent reviews that are comfortable rather than honest

**Practice method:** For each PM you manage or mentor, write a "development edge" — the one capability that, if developed, would most increase their effectiveness. Design a specific, time-bound development experience (not a training course) that would stretch that capability. Discuss it with them.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Technical products:** PM hiring must evaluate technical fluency; the most common hiring mistake is over-indexing on technical background and under-indexing on product judgment
- **Consumer products:** PM hiring must evaluate taste and consumer intuition; the most common hiring mistake is over-indexing on analytical capability
- **Enterprise products:** PM hiring must evaluate domain expertise and customer empathy; the most common hiring mistake is hiring generalists into domain-deep roles

**Industry variation:**
- **Early-stage startups:** Talent development is apprenticeship-based; formal programs are premature
- **Scale-ups:** Talent development must systematize without bureaucratizing
- **Public companies:** Talent development must include succession planning for all critical product roles

## Cluster 4: Institutional Leadership

### 4.1 Product Organization Design

The capacity to structure the product organization — roles, teams, decision rights, interfaces with other functions — to maximize the organization's ability to create value.

**Senior PM expectation:** Understands their role and decision rights within the existing organizational design. Can articulate how their team fits into the broader structure. Identifies when organizational boundaries are creating friction and raises it.

**Principal PM expectation:** Contributes to organizational design decisions at the team or group level. Identifies when team boundaries should be redrawn based on product architecture, customer segments, or strategic priorities. Can articulate the logic of different organizational models.

**Director expectation:** Designs the organizational structure for a product area. Makes explicit decisions about team boundaries, PM-to-engineering ratios, reporting structures, and interfaces with design, data, and go-to-market functions. Adapts organizational design as strategy and context change.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Designs the overall product organization structure. Determines the role of product management relative to engineering, design, data, and business functions. Makes build-buy decisions about product capabilities (insourcing, outsourcing, platforms). Ensures organizational design supports the product strategy.

**Evidence of excellence:** The organizational structure is a source of advantage, not friction. Team boundaries align with product architecture in ways that minimize coordination costs. Decision rights are clear without being rigid. The organization adapts its structure as strategy evolves.

**Common false positives:**
- Reorganizing as a substitute for strategy
- Applying a generic organizational model without adaptation to context
- Designing for the current leadership team rather than the strategy
- Creating overly complex structures (matrix organizations, dotted lines) that obscure accountability
- Optimizing for org-chart elegance rather than execution effectiveness

**Practice method:** Draw your current organizational structure. For each team boundary, identify the coordination cost it creates. For each decision type, identify who actually makes the decision (not who the org chart says makes it). Identify the single organizational change that would most reduce coordination friction without increasing other costs.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Multi-product companies:** Organizational design must balance product-specific focus against shared platform leverage
- **Platform:** The "build for internal customers" model creates unique organizational design challenges around prioritization and funding
- **Marketplace:** Organizational design often requires separate teams for supply-side and demand-side, creating coordination challenges
- **AI/ML:** Organizational design must determine where AI/ML capability lives (centralized, embedded, hybrid)

**Industry variation:**
- **Global companies:** Geographic distribution adds organizational design complexity; follow-the-sun models, regional autonomy, and cultural variation must be designed for
- **Hardware/software hybrid:** Organizational design must bridge fundamentally different development cadences and risk profiles
- **Professional services + product:** Organizational design must manage the tension between customization revenue and product leverage

---

### 4.2 Capital and Headcount Allocation

The capacity to make effective decisions about how financial and human capital is deployed across product investments.

**Senior PM expectation:** Understands the resource model for their team. Makes reasonable requests for headcount and budget. Can articulate the ROI of their initiatives. Uses resources efficiently.

**Principal PM expectation:** Builds investment cases for new initiatives. Models the resource requirements for competing approaches. Identifies when additional investment would produce disproportionate returns — and when it wouldn't. Contributes to cross-team resource allocation decisions.

**Director expectation:** Allocates headcount and budget across a product area. Makes tradeoffs between investing in existing products and funding new initiatives. Builds annual and multi-year resource plans. Manages the tension between what the strategy requires and what resources are available.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Determines the total product investment level and its allocation. Defends the product investment thesis to the board and CFO. Makes capital allocation decisions that span product development, M&A, partnerships, and platform investments. Ensures resource allocation reflects strategic priorities, not just historical inertia.

**Evidence of excellence:** Resources are concentrated on the highest-return opportunities rather than spread evenly. Investment levels change as evidence accumulates rather than being locked in annual budgets. The organization can trace resource allocation to outcomes with reasonable fidelity. Zero-based thinking is applied periodically.

**Common false positives:**
- Spreadsheet sophistication without strategic judgment
- "More resources" as the default answer to every challenge
- Allocating resources based on executive advocacy rather than opportunity
- Treating headcount as the only resource lever
- Annual planning as a negotiation exercise rather than a strategy exercise

**Practice method:** For your current resource allocation, answer: If you had to cut 20% of your budget, what would you stop? If you received 20% more, what would you start? If neither answer comes easily, the current allocation is probably not strategic.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Platform:** Investment must balance short-term feature delivery against long-term infrastructure; the "platform tax" (funding mechanism) is a critical design decision
- **New product development (zero-to-one):** Investment logic is fundamentally different from mature product optimization; ROI models are speculative
- **Multi-sided platforms:** Investment allocation must account for cross-side effects; optimizing each side independently produces bad outcomes

**Industry variation:**
- **Venture-backed:** Capital allocation is shaped by funding rounds, burn rate, and runway; the "default alive" question dominates
- **Public companies:** Capital allocation faces quarterly earnings pressure; the "rule of 40" and similar frameworks shape investment logic
- **PE-backed:** Capital allocation is shaped by the holding period and exit thesis

---

### 4.3 Board-Level Narrative

The capacity to construct and maintain the product narrative at the board level — ensuring the board has an accurate, strategically useful understanding of the product organization's performance, challenges, and opportunities.

**Senior PM expectation:** May contribute content to board materials through their leadership chain. Understands how their work connects to the company-level narrative.

**Principal PM expectation:** Provides content and narrative for board-level discussions in their domain. Can brief a director or VP for a board conversation. Understands what the board cares about and why.

**Director expectation:** Contributes directly to board materials for their product area. Can present to the board when asked. Anticipates board questions and ensures they're addressed proactively. Ensures the board narrative is accurate, not just favorable.

**VP/CPO expectation:** Owns the board-level product narrative. Determines what the board sees and how it's framed. Presents product strategy, performance, and risks to the board directly. Builds board confidence in the product organization. Manages the board's expectations about product outcomes. Navigates board dynamics — different directors have different concerns and frameworks.

**Evidence of excellence:** The board has an accurate picture of product performance — neither inflated nor unduly pessimistic. Board questions reflect genuine engagement with product strategy, not just financial oversight. The board supports product investments that require patience. Board members can articulate the product strategy in their own words.

**Common false positives:**
- Board decks that are visually impressive but substantively empty
- Telling the board what they want to hear
- Hiding bad news or dressing it up as "learning"
- Confusing board approval with board confidence
- Treating the board as an audience to be managed rather than a resource to be leveraged

**Practice method:** Write the product section of a board deck for your area as if you were the CPO. What would you want the board to know? What would you want them to decide? What would you want them to worry about? What would you not want them to worry about? Share with your manager for feedback.

**Product-archetype variation:**
- **Hardware:** Board narrative must address capital intensity, supply chain risk, and long development cycles
- **AI/ML:** Board narrative must address model strategy (build vs. use APIs), data advantages, and AI safety/governance
- **Consumer:** Board narrative must address engagement depth, not just breadth; monetization trajectory, not just user growth

**Industry variation:**
- **Pre-IPO companies:** Board narrative must support the IPO story; product metrics that matter to public market investors become salient
- **Public companies:** Board narrative must comply with disclosure obligations; materiality considerations shape what can be shared
- **Companies in turnaround:** Board narrative must balance honesty about problems with credible confidence in the path forward
- **Founder-led companies:** Board narrative must navigate the founder's relationship with the board

## How to Use This Model

### For Self-Assessment

For each capability, rate yourself honestly:
- **Developing:** You understand the concept but don't consistently practice it at your level
- **Proficient:** You practice it consistently at your current level
- **Excellent:** You practice it at the level above your current role
- **Overrated on this:** You or others believe you're stronger than you actually are

The last category is the most important and the hardest to fill in honestly.

### For Hiring and Promotion

Use the capability model as a structured interview guide. For each capability at the target level:
1. Ask the candidate to describe a specific situation that demonstrates the capability
2. Probe for the evidence of excellence indicators
3. Actively check for the false positive patterns
4. Calibrate across interviewers using the level-specific descriptors

Never accept polished narratives, prestigious backgrounds, or attractive frameworks as evidence. Insist on specific, verifiable examples.

### For Development Planning

Use the gap between current level and target level in each capability to prioritize development. A PM who is "developing" in three capabilities and "proficient" in everything else should focus on those three, not spread development effort across all capabilities.

### For Organizational Design

Use the capability requirements of your strategy to determine what kind of product talent you need. A strategy that demands deep technical fluency (platform shift, AI integration) requires different capabilities than one that demands deep customer and domain understanding (vertical SaaS expansion).

## The Anti-Patterns

These patterns are common enough, and dangerous enough, to call out explicitly:

1. **The Framework Collector:** Knows every product framework. Can facilitate a workshop on anything. Cannot point to a single consequential decision they made using any of them.

2. **The Presentation Artist:** Board decks, strategy memos, and vision statements are beautiful. Products are mediocre. Confuses communicating well about products with building good products.

3. **The Process Builder:** Responds to every organizational challenge by creating a process. The organization becomes better at following process and worse at exercising judgment.

4. **The Credentialed Generalist:** Prestigious background, strong analytical skills, excellent communication. Has never stayed anywhere long enough to live with the consequences of their decisions.

5. **The Feature Baron:** Ships a lot. Measures output, not outcomes. Cannot answer "what did you decide not to build and why?"

6. **The Strategy Tourist:** Develops strategies that are intellectually interesting and practically irrelevant. Cannot connect the strategy to any specific resource allocation or prioritization decision.

7. **The Empathy Performer:** Talks eloquently about users. Has not had a direct, unmediated customer conversation in months.

8. **The Data Alchemist:** Uses data to justify decisions already made. Can find statistical support for any position. Never encountered data that changed their mind.

9. **The Consensus Seeker:** Gets everyone aligned. Makes no enemies. Also makes no hard decisions. Alignment without substance is compliance, not commitment.

10. **The Survivor:** Has survived at one company for a long time. Confuses organizational knowledge with product leadership capability. May be excellent at the internal game and mediocre at everything else.
