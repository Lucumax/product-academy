# 01 — Core Doctrine

## Purpose

Core doctrine is the foundational layer of the Product Leadership Academy. It contains the principles and frameworks that apply across product archetypes, industries, and organizational stages. If all other modules were stripped away, the content in this module should be sufficient for a product leader to reason about the most common and consequential product decisions.

## Structure

| File | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| `README.md` | This orientation |

## What Makes a Principle "Canonical"?

A principle qualifies as canonical doctrine when it meets these criteria:

1. **Evidence-backed.** Supported by at least one Tier A source or multiple corroborated Tier B sources. No principle rests on Tier C or lower evidence alone.
2. **Cross-cutting.** Applies across multiple product archetypes, industries, and organizational stages. Principles that apply only to a specific context belong in the relevant overlay module.
3. **Actionable.** A product leader can use the principle to make a specific decision or evaluate a specific trade-off. "Put the customer first" is not a principle — it is a value. "Customer requests should be evaluated against product strategy, not prioritized individually" is a principle.
4. **Falsifiable.** The principle includes conditions under which it does NOT apply and conditions under which it should be reversed. Principles without counter-conditions are dogmas.
5. **Owned.** The principle includes a practice exercise and a practical tool. Principles without application are academic.

## Source Standards

Every canonical principle must meet the evidence standards defined in `SOURCE_POLICY.md`:

- **Tier A required** for any principle that makes a strong causal claim. Example: "Empowered teams produce measurably better outcomes than directed teams" requires Tier A evidence.
- **Tier B + corroboration** may support a principle that makes a weaker claim. Example: "Empowered teams tend to produce higher team satisfaction than directed teams" requires Tier B with corroboration.
- **Tier C and below** may inform principles but cannot serve as primary evidence.

## Decision Frameworks

Decision frameworks are structured approaches to specific product decisions: prioritization, build-vs-buy, pricing, platform strategy, sunset decisions, and so on. A framework must include:

1. When to use it (and when not to)
2. Step-by-step application
3. Common failure modes
4. An example application
5. A practice exercise

Frameworks are not algorithms. They are structured thinking tools that reduce the variance in decision quality across an organization. A good framework makes a good decision more likely; it does not guarantee one.

## Maintenance

- Principles are reviewed semi-annually for evidence updates
- New sources that strengthen or challenge a principle are added to the evidence or counterevidence arrays
- Confidence ratings are adjusted as evidence accumulates
- Principles that cannot meet the evidence standard after two review cycles are demoted to "practitioner guidance" (not canonical)
