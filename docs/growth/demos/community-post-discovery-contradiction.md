When interviews say users want a feature but usage data disagrees — which evidence should win?

A concrete case from a (fictional) fitness app:

- 25 user interviews, 22 positive about the "habit streaks" feature.
- 5 showcase users rave: "streaks are why I keep coming back."
- Usage: 6% of daily active users ever touch streaks. Weekly sessions flat at 3.1.

Interviews say invest. Usage says almost nobody uses it.

The common failure here is picking a side: either "listen to your users" (enthusiasm wins) or
"trust the data" (usage wins), then arguing about it forever.

A different framing — treat them as two different kinds of evidence:

- What users say (stated intent) tells you what they *think* they want.
- What users do (behavior) tells you what they actually do.

For a claim about why people come back, stated intent is a loyalty signal, not a mechanism.
The loudest voices are usually the most engaged cohort — the one already using the product.

So the honest conclusion isn't "invest" and it isn't "ignore." It's: we cannot tell yet,
and here's the cheapest test that tells us — compare retention for streak-users vs
non-streak-users. If streaks cause retention, the cohort split shows it. If streaks merely
accompany the already-engaged, the split shows that too.

That's one proposed method: treat interviews and usage as different evidence types, and
design the discriminating test instead of taking sides. [This is the shape of the
`synthesize-customer-discovery` skill we're building — link.]

Where does this fall down? I want the criticism:

- When is stated enthusiasm the right signal to trust over behavior?
- When is the cohort split not enough — what other confounders am I missing?
- What would you do differently with the same 25 interviews and 6% usage number?

I'll take the honest failures over agreement. If you've seen this trap — either direction —
I'd like to hear how it played out.
